### (Podcast) EPA 608 Changes - What You Need to Know w⧸ Jeremy Arling  
*Source id: 6yJQiji1LH4*

- Effective Jan 1 2018, HFCs (R-410A, etc.) are handled like ozone-depleting refrigerants: you must be 608-certified to open/service an HFC appliance, use EPA-certified recovery equipment, hit the same evacuation levels, and be certified to purchase (the distributor verifies).
- Currently-certified techs do NOT have to recertify (the test bank is just being updated), but because the rules changed, what you learned years ago is now partly outdated.
- New RECORDKEEPING (not reporting) for disposing of appliances with 5+ lbs: per-appliance (company name, location, date, refrigerant type), monthly recovery totals, and per-transaction amounts sent for reclamation/destruction - kept by the COMPANY, not the individual tech (the old recovery-equipment reporting requirement was eliminated).
- Leak-repair rules (effective Jan 1 2019) apply only to appliances with 50+ lbs; thresholds were lowered (comfort cooling 15%->10%, commercial refrigeration 35%->30%, industrial 35%->20%) and now require a 608 tech to do a leak inspection plus initial and follow-up verification tests, with records provided to the owner.

### (Podcast) What it Takes to be an Excellent Residential Service Tech  
*Source id: nB4NUkpCsEk*

- Technical competence is the foundation (understand and repair the equipment); soft skills don't substitute for it. On top of it are ten traits: observant, resourceful, pleasant, positive, organized, efficient, conscientious, self-aware, and neat/clean/communicative.
- Wide-narrow-wide diagnosis: first assume the system was set up or is running improperly and inspect EVERYTHING (wide), then narrow to the fault to quote and fix, then go wide again to test that it runs properly.
- Be resourceful - read manuals, panel data tags, and core books (RACT, the RSES manual, Dick Wirz, Carter Stanfield) rather than calling people or guessing; being observant plus resourceful is how you escape Dunning-Kruger.
- End every call by asking 'is there anything I could do or have done better for you?' - having the customer voice that it was great makes the whole experience feel better and reduces the sense they were taken for a ride.

### 5 Service Strategies to Prevent HVAC Callbacks  
*Source id: Zc28AlF-68I*

- Five memorizable service callback-prevention strategies: (1) ask questions (read dispatch notes, property history, talk to the customer); (2) quote or clean the drain every time; (3) remove all panels (check everything, including the filter); (4) enter the readings (document); (5) do a final walkthrough - 'touch everything'.
- The final walkthrough is the single most important callback preventer even though it is an 'unnecessary' extra step ~90% of the time - it catches the costly 10%.
- The number one service callback is a clogged drain, so quote it or clean it on every service call whether you think it needs it or not.

### Buyer Beware - Is It Illegal For a Tech to Recharge Your A⧸C With R22？  
*Source id: yhACu6o6oms*

- It is legal to recharge a residential air conditioning system (under 50 lb of refrigerant) with R-22; the EPA Section 608 code does not prohibit it, and techs who claim otherwise are misinformed.
- Field mixing / topping off a system with a refrigerant other than what is already in it is illegal - the EPA does not allow field mixing under any circumstances.
- Whether to recharge R-22 is an economic and environmental decision, not a legal one; a small top-off (about a pound or under) can make sense to restore efficiency even while planning a leak repair.

### Callback Prevention - Communication and Priorities  
*Source id: oZZb7pgp-1c*

- A callback is any time the customer calls back within a reasonable window (about 30 days); many are driven by unrealistic expectations or the customer feeling unheard, so customer communication prevents them.
- When a customer raises a concern, acknowledge and repeat it back before explaining anything; take measurements first, then talk about why.
- Commission the system properly: verify staging/dip switches/controller setup, and address airflow first via visual inspection and static pressure before focusing on charge.

### Callback Prevention w⧸ Bert  
*Source id: eY030giK9_w*

- The three pillars behind preventing callbacks (and being a great technician) are mindset, habits, and knowledge/skills, and mindset is the most important of the three.

### Charging and Testing Heat Pumps in Heat Mode  
*Source id: AC7Ur2EILbs*

- Charge in cooling / weigh it in (most accurate), then CHECK in heat mode against the manufacturer's heat-mode CHECK chart; a check chart is not a charging chart, just as you check superheat but don't charge by it.
- Per-foot charge is tiny, so being 5-10 ft off on line length barely affects operation; on long/uncertain line sets do all your checks now but schedule a cooling-season recheck.
- Every gas furnace install should be commissioned: confirm airflow, run a combustion analysis, and test gas pressure; if you aren't trained on those three, get someone scheduled to do them.

### Commercial HVAC Drain Cleaning： Pro Tips That Prevent Callbacks  
*Source id: DMo1FtdNWeg*

- The best drain-cleaning method is a high-CFM (corded) vacuum at the very outlet of the drain, pulling as much of the line as you can; battery vacuums are the next best, and the drain pan (where the water starts) is the other end you must clean.
- Manufacturers say use water (not cleaners) on condensers; never use chemicals on microchannel coils, and if you use cleaner on evaporator coils you must rinse it or it ruins the coil over time.
- Spray drain-pan treatment as a thin film along the sides of a dried pan, not poured - pouring or over-applying forms a sludge ball; don't spray it down the drain line (not its intended use).

### Commercial VS. Residential HVAC⧸R  
*Source id: Xu4gtqZRiyY*

- Decide early which segment suits you: residential/light commercial (most of the market and most trade-school training), commercial, or industrial; principles like electrical diagnosis, the refrigeration circuit, and airflow translate across all of them.
- Residential service demands strong interpersonal and customer-service skills (and price/money conversations), so pay varies widely with how good you are with people; commercial/industrial is more technical and mechanical with larger, more dangerous equipment and fewer conflicts.

### Commercial vs. Residential A⧸C w⧸ AK HVAC  
*Source id: r_4awK8MvIc*

- Commercial/heavy equipment demands a broader, always-learning technical mindset (application-based custom equipment, far more controls and environmental controls) but generally needs fewer people/sales/customer-service skills than residential.
- If you want to own your own business, residential is the more realistic path (you learn soft skills, money conversations, and have more influence as a senior tech); commercial-to-residential moves are usually people making the jump to entrepreneurship.
- To earn the most on the commercial side, specialize in cutting-edge or less-saturated equipment (VRF, controls, specific chillers) - you become the highest-paid and most in-demand because you can fix what others can't.

### Condensing Unit Install Practices (RSES NATE Prep)  
*Source id: oaLdbma78Lc*

- Outdoor-unit install best practices (RSES/NATE prep): check for transportation damage and verify nameplate voltage before installing; single-phase equipment rated 230 V +/-10% should read below ~253 V, and 208 V ratings have less low-side tolerance.
- Modern R-410A systems use POE oil that is hygroscopic, so keep the system clean, dry, and tight - purge and flow nitrogen while brazing, pull a deep vacuum, replace liquid-line filter driers whenever the system is open, and break a triple evacuation with dry nitrogen, not air.
- Locate the condenser correctly (space for airflow/service, above snow line, away from windows/doors, dryer/kitchen vents, and roof drainage) on a solid level, anchored pad; use ACR tubing (not plumbing copper) and proper filler/flux.

### Dealing with Upset Customers： HVAC De-escalation Strategies  
*Source id: gGZcydG6tQg*

- De-escalate by hearing the customer out, showing empathy and finding something to agree with even when they're wrong; don't fight fire with fire.
- Emotional venting is a pressure-relief valve; people often just want to be heard, so listening and finding common ground is the most powerful move.
- Don't take it personally: the emotion the customer brings usually isn't about the actual HVAC issue but their backstory (financial, family, being hot in their home).

### Duct Cleaning Training with Zach Ortwine  
*Source id: ENUw0nG4f9k*

- Duct cleaning is not a cure-all; set realistic expectations. It knocks off non-adhered dry contaminants, but sticky/wet residues won't come out, and cleaning can temporarily increase duct leakage.
- Duct cleaning must run the system under negative pressure (suction) while agitating dust toward the vacuum; brush-and-shopvac systems used wrong loosen dust that then blows all over the home.
- Never call growth 'mold' (say 'suspect microbial growth') without a lab test; don't say 'no' either. For duct board with heavy growth, replace it rather than coat it, and never fog duck board (it damages it).

### EPA Regulation Uncertainty  
*Source id: 0HbnhO7Gkqo*

- The EPA proposed (2018) rolling back the 2016 rule's leak-rate and repair mandates for HFC/HFO refrigerants and sought comment on rescinding all of Subpart F for any refrigerant that doesn't deplete the ozone, based on an ambiguous reading of the Clean Air Act (608(a) mentions class 1/2 substances; 608(c) prohibits venting substitutes).
- Rescinding Subpart F for HFCs would create a state where venting is illegal but recovery, leak repair, and technician certification are NOT required - no guidance on legal behavior, anyone could buy refrigerant in any quantity.
- Constantly changing regulations reduce compliance and dissemination, risk a state-by-state patchwork, and threaten reclaimer jobs (no reclaim requirement means techs reuse R410A/R134A/R404A instead of sending it in).

### Educating & Training Gen Z w⧸ Ty Branaman  
*Source id: PHs9AD6wRz8*

- 'Nobody wants to work anymore' has been said by every generation (documented quotes from 1905, 1907, 1916, 1937, 1952, 1979, 1999, 2014); the difference is each generation grows up in a different environment, so understanding Gen Z (25 and under) starts with empathy, not judgment.
- Gen Z wants compensation and a growth path (they've seen college debt and unstable economies), constant/specific feedback, the 'why' before being told what to do, respect as a baseline, and the ability to build their own story - so cross-train them (install 4 days, ride with maintenance/service/dispatch on the 5th) and give a clear path up.

### Electrification Panel w⧸ Bill Spohn, Jim Bergmann and Kimberly Llewellyn  
*Source id: ZNqe40qPJLY*

- Electrification is an irreversible trend but 'not like flipping a switch' — the equipment is ready, but the industry's technician skill set is the biggest barrier to doing it successfully at mass adoption.
- For existing/poorly-built homes in cold climates, dual-fuel/hybrid systems make sense; all-electric works best in new construction designed from the outset with very low loads and cold-climate heat pumps sized for design conditions.
- Electrification will fail unless the industry fixes duct leakage (one screw hole ~1 CFM), reads install manuals, follows existing (voluntary) standards, and makes good flares — training, not products, is the core problem.

### Estimating Tips and Software  
*Source id: kRMTd5qvaPs*

- Good estimating merges the quantitative (counting widgets/materials to find the true cost) with the heuristic/qualitative (subject-matter judgment about level of effort, commissioning time, and site conditions) — counting widgets alone leaves a big chunk of effort off the table.
- The quantitative counts get you ~80% of the way there; the other ~20% depends on experience, past-job numbers, and zooming out — but be brutally honest about real production values and real expenses rather than assuming you'll magically go 20% faster.
- Estimating should transfer value into production, not just produce a final number — an assembly-based on-screen takeoff (each dot = a box of widgets: pipe, insulation, clamps, strut, etc.) can guide how the job is actually built and bought out.

### Fix Your Customer 1st, Then Fix Their System with Andy Holt  
*Source id: xgkWl2eXhPs*

- Fix the customer first, then fix their system: customers never compliment your technical skills, only how you made them feel while you did them (credited to Ron Smith) — 'we're not in the HVAC business, we're in the people business, we just happen to turn wrenches' (adapted from Truett Cathy).
- Approach every call from the customer's point of view: the average customer has never bought a replacement system, so they're nervous — the whole service call from phone answer to departure is a chain of nonverbal reassurance (pick up within two rings, call before arriving, smile, gratitude, eye contact).
- The customer is part of the system: even with all green flags on MeasureQuick, a supply blowing 86F heat-pump air onto a chair leaves the customer cold, so you must diagnose comfort (mixed-air/discharge temp, register direction), not just measured performance.

### HVAC Install Process Improvement： How to Eliminate Surprises on Every Job  
*Source id: SwLf140VfVo*

- Install surprises come from poor PROCESS, not bad or lazy people; 'poor planning process prevents poor planning.' Break prep into three time buckets: at the shop (scope/de-scope the proposal, stage equipment, breakers/permits), the night before/after the last job (restock truck consumables like wire nuts and spray foam), and at the job (pre-work huddle, walk the job, double-check measurements/access, open boxes early).
- The two unforgivable install mistakes are refrigerant leaks and water/drain leaks -- either one erases weeks of good, clean-looking work in cost. Prevent leaks by pressure testing to an EXACT pressure (no 'about 120') and laying bubbles on flat, checking every joint (4-8 per install) with a mirror for micro-leaks, because the tiny leaks get you, not the big hissing ones.
- Use checklists -- not to make robots, but so nothing is missed and the next person can be trained (surgeons and nuclear-sub crews follow checklists even when expert). Treat missing measurements/photos as seriously as failing an inspection or leaving a leak.

### HVAC Safety Training for Technicians ｜ Ladders, PPE, Lockout Tagout & More  
*Source id: dRzbyWDUZ8o*

- Most incidents don't happen because people don't know better - they happen because we choose not to do what we know (seat belts, PPE, tie-offs) due to overconfidence, being rushed, and never having seen the consequence.
- Ladder safety: 100% tie off (the transition on/off the roof is the most dangerous moment), and set an extension ladder to the proper 77.5-degree angle using the free NIOSH ladder app on your phone.
- PPE sits at the lowest level of the hierarchy of controls because it depends entirely on you actually wearing it; gloves and eye protection can't live in the truck. Be aware of stored/potential energy: electrical (lock out/tag out, capacitors), thermal (hot discharge lines/panels in sun), mechanical (blower wheels, fan blades, belts), gravitational (falling tools), and pressure (Schrader cores).

### HVAC School Admin Discussion - Moderating a Successful Community  
*Source id: cygA-0Bt9KQ*

- A good professional online community (the ~42,000-member HVAC School Facebook group) is worth running because it keeps you relevant and learning, exposes you to other regions' practices (Bryan says he couldn't do the podcast/YouTube without it), and is a low-pressure place to practice your 'customer voice.'
- Moderation standards exist to keep it productive: swearing is discouraged not because the word offends but because it escalates fast and can slide into personal attacks - the line is what you're DOING with the language (explanatory vs attacking); enforce rules but with grace, and don't engage angry DMs.
- Be genuinely helpful: think before answering, don't fire off one-word/shortest-answer 'it's a piece of junk' replies, add regional context (there's no national HVAC code), cite sources/link the HVAC School tech tips, and only answer when you're fairly confident you're right.

### HVAC Service VS. Installation  
*Source id: Jxd8orulud4*

- Service and installation/projects aren't better or worse - they're different: installation/projects are more physically taxing (ductwork, copper, fabrication), while service carries more mental strain (complex diagnosis, customer interaction, being responsible for outcomes, and an on-call cycle with nights/weekends).
- Installers/project people tend to be 'workmen' who like how things look, work neatly and focus on efficiency and physical tools (saw, metal brake); service techs tend to be more technical/problem-solving and prefer meters and gauges - Bryan places himself in the technical camp.
- People often start on the project side young and later transition to the less physically demanding service side, but that transition requires developing customer-service, communication and technical (the 'why') skills; retail/replacement technicians sit in between with service-like customer skills but more fixed schedules.

### HVAC Summer Service Calls： Rushing, Mental Fog & How to Stay on Your Game  
*Source id: ud7ksq-g_Ts*

- Summer means more hours, more calls, more heat/exhaustion and mental fog, all creating a desire to rush - and rushing equals mistakes AND missed opportunities (both callbacks and lower average tickets).
- 'Sharpen your axe' before summer: truly know your tools. Be 100% confident in your leak detector (e.g. learn the finicky H10 - calibration vial, tube airflow, sensor, heat adjustment) and verify your vacuum pump by putting a micron gauge right on it (should pull below 100 microns in a couple minutes; if not, usually change the oil). Doing these checks regularly builds a statistical baseline so you know when something's off.
- Rushing collapses the wide-narrow-wide process into all-narrow: you fix the capacitor/drain and leave, missing the whole-house picture and the second 'wide' (verify, document, communicate). Don't punt or say 'keep an eye on it' - fully resolve the problem or give a clear documented path forward.

### HVAC Training Life #BertLife

### HVAC vs. Refrigeration - Which is Best for You？  
*Source id: WWNxrUyJINw*

- When we say refrigeration we mean commercial refrigeration (grocery/market/restaurant boxes); residential refrigerators/dishwashers/washers are appliance repair, a separate category; HVAC can be commercial, residential or industrial and is mostly comfort-focused.
- On the HVAC/comfort side you often make instantaneous repairs the customer feels immediately; refrigeration requires patience because you wait for the box to pull down, for defrost, for ice to harvest, so it suits detail-oriented, patient people.
- Refrigeration carries higher stakes (thousands to hundreds of thousands in product loss), nastier on-call schedules, heavier components requiring rigging, and more manufacturer-specific training since schools rarely teach market refrigeration controls.

### Head Trash  
*Source id: tFLrb5j449Q*

- 'Head trash' is a story that builds on itself and, like a black hole, pulls in other stories/grievances until you've built a whole narrative about another person's motives that is mostly assumptions, not facts.
- The first step is recognizing everyone has it (you and the people you work with); then examine how many things in the story are actual facts versus read-ins to someone else's intent.

## Contrarian takes (where Bryan / guests diverge from common teaching)

- **Common teaching:** I'll have to retake my EPA 608 exam.
  **Bryan's position:** No - currently-certified techs don't recertify.
  **Reasoning:** Only the test bank is being updated; but the rules changed, so relearn them.
  **Source:** [(Podcast) EPA 608 Changes - What You Need to Know w⧸ Jeremy Arling] (id: 6yJQiji1LH4)

- **Common teaching:** It's illegal to recharge a system under 50 lbs or an old R22/CFC system.
  **Bryan's position:** No - existing properly-functioning equipment can be recharged and serviced.
  **Reasoning:** Leak-repair requirements only apply at 50+ lbs; but R22 availability/cost rises (phaseout 2020, recovered/recycled only).
  **Source:** [(Podcast) EPA 608 Changes - What You Need to Know w⧸ Jeremy Arling] (id: 6yJQiji1LH4)

- **Common teaching:** Propane (r22a) is a legal drop-in for R22.
  **Bryan's position:** No - r22a/flammable retrofits are illegal and dangerous.
  **Reasoning:** Appliances have exploded on techs; propane is only allowed in small factory-charged hermetically-sealed appliances.
  **Source:** [(Podcast) EPA 608 Changes - What You Need to Know w⧸ Jeremy Arling] (id: 6yJQiji1LH4)

- **Common teaching:** Just fix 'a' leak (new caps) to comply.
  **Bryan's position:** Now a 608 tech must inspect and verify the system no longer leaks above threshold.
  **Reasoning:** The leak inspection + verification tests ensure the repair is done correctly.
  **Source:** [(Podcast) EPA 608 Changes - What You Need to Know w⧸ Jeremy Arling] (id: 6yJQiji1LH4)

- **Common teaching:** You can top off R22 with 438a/407c.
  **Bryan's position:** Mixing refrigerants is illegal (it creates an unapproved refrigerant).
  **Reasoning:** All refrigerants must be EPA-approved; mixing creates something new and unpredictable.
  **Source:** [(Podcast) EPA 608 Changes - What You Need to Know w⧸ Jeremy Arling] (id: 6yJQiji1LH4)

- **Common teaching:** Being blunt/honest excuses being unpleasant.
  **Bryan's position:** No - you can be honest and blunt with a smile; be pleasant.
