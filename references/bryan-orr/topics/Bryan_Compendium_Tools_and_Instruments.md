### (Podcast) Brazing Basics and Tips  
*Source id: Hcp_ExcxvO0*

- Brazing safety is central: know your oxy-acetylene gear, inspect hoses/regulators, wear safety glasses and gloves, keep damp rags/water and a fire extinguisher, and protect the space (cover/relocate smoke detectors, watch for fire sprinklers, protect carpet).
- Keep contaminants out of the copper: ream and deburr with the open end pointed down, clean with emery/sand cloth then wipe with a dry microfiber, and make tight, properly-reduced Copper-to-Copper fits (use reducers/a swage tool) rather than bridging a size gap with rod.
- Purge then flow a whisper of nitrogen (2-5 scfh) while brazing to prevent internal carbon/oxidation scale, always leaving a port open so pressure can relieve; set torch pressures at the regulator (equal oxygen and acetylene), use the correct tip size for the job, light acetylene first / shut oxygen first, and braze with a neutral-to-slightly-carbonizing flame (never oxidizing).
- Make the joint by heating the tube to draw solder INTO the fitting (don't just bridge the edge), cap the fillet, and verify with three layers: visual/mirror, nitrogen bubble test, then a micron vacuum decay.

### AL-COP Braze - Brazing Aluminum to Copper  
*Source id: I8QYhLHCW_c*

- Al-Cop braze (SolderWeld) joins aluminum to copper and flows at ~950-1000F, in the safe zone below aluminum's ~1200F disappearing point and standard copper rods' ~1200F flow point.
- Control heat by working PRIMARILY on the COPPER (in the safe zone) - you overheat/lose aluminum by overheating the copper.
- It's a flux-channel rod (non-acidic flux laid in the channel); lay it flux-side-down so the flux wets and pulls the alloy into the joint.

### Aluminum to Copper Pressure Test w⧸ Al-Cop  
*Source id: h4buj67I-VU*

- Al-Cop aluminum-to-copper joints hold under pressure: demonstrated pressure tests to 300 and 500 psi with no bubbles/leaks in both the standard joint and a solder-ring joint.
- You can pre-form solder rings (bend Al-Cop around a same-diameter fitting) for tight field applications, but ring joints need MORE complete heat (otherwise the solder melts and falls off before the joint reaches temperature).
- Swage the aluminum and fit the copper inside it: copper's higher heat conductance draws the solder into the joint; a snug, very tight clearance and clean metals are the two biggest factors.

### Analog & Digital Manifolds  
*Source id: 1GE2yNIW268*

- Neither camp is fully right: analog is fine for non-critically-charged work, recovery (won't contaminate good sensors), and is cheaper; digital wins on accuracy and resolution, which matters on critically-charged systems (ductless, micro-channel, ice machines) where a couple ounces is a big percentage of a small internal volume.
- For ductless where you weigh in charge (not setting superheat/subcool), you don't need high-accuracy gauges - the manifold just controls refrigerant in/out; the temperature side (suction/discharge temp) matters more, plus a good scale, calculator, and pencil.
- Understand your tool: accuracy (is the reading correct, e.g. plus/minus 1 psi vs 0.4 psi) vs resolution (digits after the decimal) - high resolution with poor accuracy is a farce; you must zero out and calibrate temperature probes in ice water.

### Braze or Solder Anything in HVAC⧸R  
*Source id: 1HLpV45pyY8*

- The SolderWeld HVAC multi-kit covers most joining jobs: 15% (or 5%) silver for copper-to-copper, 56% flux-coated rod for dissimilar metals (copper-steel/brass, burned-through compressor plating, TXVs, reversing valves), AlloySol for aluminum rub-out patches, and AlCopBraze (channel-flux) for aluminum, copper, and copper-to-aluminum.
- More silver = more ductile and free-flowing and better against vibration/temperature/pressure swings; Bryan uses 15% across the board as 'cheap insurance' and reserves it for high-stress spots like a compressor discharge line.
- The number-one brazing mistake is not applying enough heat fast enough - techs afraid of burning through wave the torch around and never reach copper's slight cherry-red color change; move the torch IN and OUT and draw the alloy INTO the joint (the outside cap weld is secondary).

### Brazing Heat Protection with Viper Wetrag  
*Source id: bbgziEvklhI*

- Electronic expansion valves have heat-sensitive PLASTIC internal components; when brazing a connection a few inches away, the heat travels back down the pipe and can melt/warp them - always protect them.
- Viper WetRag molds to the component, is reusable, and shields it (demo held the valve cool to the touch and still modulating even while the copper was heated to ~1600 F); an unprotected valve melted and warped its plastic.
- There's nothing worse than failing the new component while installing it - protect heat-sensitive devices (EEVs, temperature sensors, anything in the line).

### Brazing Required Practices with Roman Baugh Part 1  
*Source id: ZvFiPGp6l0M*

- Torch tip selection is not optional: the tip number is the orifice size, which sets fuel flow and heat - too small a tip (00) can't heat inch-and-1/8, too big (2) overheats 1/4-inch. Every manufacturer publishes a tip-size-to-pipe-size chart, and bigger tips need higher regulator pressure and burn more fuel.
- Match the alloy to the base metals and know their temperatures: copper melts ~1950 F, aluminum ~1220 F, and 15% silphos flows at 1300 F / fully liquid at 1475 F, so you work copper in a ~1600 F sweet spot; preheat copper before aluminum when joining the two.
- The fitting's insertion depth equals the pipe diameter and the thin gap is the filler gap for capillary action - a wallowed-out loose fitting lets the alloy run past into the tube.

### Brazing Required Practices with Roman Baugh Part 2  
*Source id: DPGiMU2diDw*

- Light the torch oxygen-FIRST (crack it low, use a finger over the tip to hear the hiss), then add acetylene slowly as you strike - lighting acetylene alone makes a dirty, sooty burn; shut off fuel (acetylene) first, then oxygen.
- Use a neutral flame (balanced, rounded primary cone) ~90% of the time; the hottest point is the tip of the secondary feather. A quiet 'whisper' flame is for small pipe (1/4, 3/8) and a louder flame (more fuel/heat) for larger pipe.
- A joint can look perfectly sealed on the outside but be a hollow cap with no penetration - the #1 cause of these hidden bad joints is insufficient heat; you must preheat the copper to cherry red (orange = too hot/melting) and heat the joint base so capillary attraction draws the alloy all the way to the unseen backside.

### Brazing Techniques  
*Source id: QBvANI3hyB4*

- JOINING (tube-to-tube) draws the alloy INTO the joint by capillary action - you must get the whole joint hot enough to turn the alloy fully liquid; REPAIRING (e.g. patching an aluminum coil rub-out) must NOT draw alloy in (that blocks the tube) - different alloy, different technique.
- Black inside a tube is cupric oxide from oxygen, NOT an overheated joint (an overheated joint is a melted hole); the average pipefitter's real error is not getting the joint hot enough - just sealing the edge.
- Copper-to-copper needs no flux because the phosphorus in silphos/faz-copper is the fluxing agent; the moment you join dissimilar metals (copper-steel/brass) you must switch to a different alloy AND a flux (no phosphorus on steel).

### Brazing With and Without Flowing Nitrogen + Using Silver Solder Rings  
*Source id: 5wUAKR8Lj9c*

- Flowing nitrogen at a very low rate (3-5 SCFM - so gentle you can't feel it) while brazing keeps the inside of the tube clean; without it you make black scale/flakes that break loose and clog metering devices.
- The hotter you get the tubing, the more flakes you make - so heat control matters even beyond nitrogen; if the oxygen is fully displaced the joint stays clean and heat control is less critical for scale.
- With silver solder rings (and regular solder), good heat control AND placement are essential: if the ring gets too hot it won't wick UP into the joint and gravity pulls it DOWN instead.

### Can You Discharge a Capacitor Using a Voltmeter？  
*Source id: H3eyBGy2x1I*

- A voltmeter can tell you whether a capacitor is holding a charge, but it is not an effective way to discharge one in a timely fashion (it can take up to about half an hour).
- Use a meter to verify voltage and a dedicated capacitor discharge tool to actually discharge the capacitor.
- Capacitors are usually not charged when you arrive, so checking with a meter first is a fine, safe practice.

### Charging and Recovering with measureQuick  
*Source id: 32vCt2xQrp0*

### Cleaning BARD units with Venom Packs  
*Source id: AHHZKB0LcSw*

- Bard and Marvair wall-hung units (schools, telecom shelters, portable buildings) get dirty fast because of small flat-panel coil surface area and high air velocities; the condenser coil especially needs frequent maintenance.

### Cleaning Large Coils with CoilPro  
*Source id: bmP59Ke2SWs*

### Coil Cleaning with John Pastorello  
*Source id: AoG75swnZVI*

- Acid coil cleaners attack both copper and aluminum and break the bond on the coil (loose fins, poor heat transfer); alkaline cleaners eat aluminum but not copper; the trend is toward safer near-neutral cleaners with corrosion inhibitors.
- Foam cleaning is the most efficient method because soil bonds to surfaces at many contact angles and the fine bubbles hit all the angles; build foam from the bottom up so it holds in place for maximum contact time.
- Dwell time and a two-wash cycle matter most: the first wash loosens bonds, the second (at a higher dilution) releases the soil; brushing with a soft brush breaks the bond between soil and metal.

### Copper Deburring： Comparing Tools and Techniques  
*Source id: nor8HNgnfmY*

- Burrs create turbulence that adds equivalent length, reduces efficiency, adds refrigerant noise, and drops oil out of the refrigerant (hurting oil return); you want laminar not turbulent flow.
- Deburring tool and reamer both beat needlenose pliers, but any tool used aggressively gouges the copper and creates shavings/out-of-round; technique matters more than the tool.
- Deburr before flaring: undeburred or damaged copper cracks when flared and a thinned/scratched flare face becomes the gasket that leaks.

### Ductless Maintenance Steps - Part 1  
*Source id: 67hxY57snqU*

- Ductless systems require far more cleaning than unitary - especially the blower wheel - and a good ductless maintenance takes longer, so price/label the blower-wheel pull-and-clean as separate labor and educate the customer on it.

### Flaring and Preventing Leaks on Ductless Systems  
*Source id: 3xtMrhO-TtY*

- A good non-leaking flare requires: square clean cut, proper deburr/ream (not too little, not so much you thin and crack the edge), flare nut on first, tube pre-bent so tension pushes into the connection, a nice round smooth flare face, and torqued to spec.
- The flare goal is maximum contact area between a clean smooth round flare face and the cone — not too small, not so big it hits the flare-nut threads. Make the flare, then hold it against the cone to confirm it covers the surface, sits flat, and doesn't extend past or recede inside the edge.
- On ductless installs, pressurize higher than typical (400-500 PSI, Mitsubishi historically said 600), let it sit ~30 minutes, and bubble test flat then re-inspect with a mirror and flashlight for microfoam — because ductless has no TXV (its electronic expansion valve is in the condenser), so you're only pressurizing tubing and coil.

### Gas & Combustion Tools  
*Source id: T6Dz3sb8ocI*

- A combustion analyzer measures temperature, oxygen and CO after combustion; it does NOT measure CO2 directly. ### HVAC Gauges, Analog vs. Digital vs. Probes  
*Source id: MxMaU0hnx2w*

- Analog manifolds are a great TEACHING tool (tactile feel, manual superheat/subcool) but have poor resolution and accuracy (easily 3-4 psi off), which matters on microchannel/critical-charge/ice-machine/refrigeration work.

### HVAC Multimeter Skills： Continuity vs. Resistance & Diode Testing  
*Source id: VR7BdPdTT7o*

- Continuity and resistance both measure resistance, but continuity has a tiny range/resolution (roughly 0-50 ohms, some 0-100 or under 10) and is meant for checking circuits that SHOULD be connected (switches, closed contactors). Resistance (ohms) reads the whole scale (0 to ~50 MOhm).
- To check a grounded/failed compressor use RESISTANCE, not continuity: manufacturers (Copeland/Daikin/Mitsubishi) call anything below ~1 MOhm a grounded compressor, but continuity only 'sees' the bottom ~50 ohms, so a fault in the kOhm-MOhm range is invisible on continuity. Fan motors (any phase) are windings like compressors -- same rule.
- A diode is an electrical check valve (one-way); the meter's diode-check function forces it open/closed by pushing current through it. Use it on inverter/rectifier boards with NO high voltage applied (it will blow the meter/fuse); reverse or stuck readings vs the service manual mean a bad diode.

### How to Braze Air Conditioning Copper  
*Source id: ikNqUTK_HGg*

- Brazing copper-to-copper for AC: remove Schraders, clean and deburr the copper, fit it snug (use bushings/reducers for gaps), then PURGE nitrogen and FLOW nitrogen (2-5 SCFH, a whisper) throughout brazing to prevent internal oxides.
- Use a 15% silver-phosphorus rod (the phosphorus self-fluxes on copper); heat the male portion first to dark cherry, then the whole joint, to draw the alloy INTO the joint - don't just cap it.
- If you burn through the copper plating on a copper-plated steel valve, switch to a high-silver rod without phosphorus (e.g. 56% flux-coated) because phosphorus doesn't react well with steel.

### How to Braze Copper to Steel  
*Source id: J0keW0INlQg*

- Brazing copper to steel (common when a compressor's copper plating is burned off exposing steel) uses a 56% silver flux-coated rod (SolderWeld Sil-Sol 56), which also does steel-to-steel, brass-to-copper, etc.
- Apply heat to the STEEL side (higher thermal mass and higher melting point); it conducts into the copper, and when the copper turns dark cherry the flux flows and pulls the alloy around/into the joint.
- Flow nitrogen (2-6 SCFH, barely audible) throughout, and clean the acidic flux off with water/steel brush after the joint cools.

### Multimeter 101 for HVAC 3D  
*Source id: fa8NM7JzISM*

- Ohms measures resistance between two de-energized points; always do a lead-to-lead ohm test first. Zero ohms = closed/perfect path, infinite (OL) = open. For own measurements to be useful you must know the expected value (from specs, a good device, or experience).
- On a compressor: terminal-to-terminal reads ohms; terminal-to-ground should read OL/mega-ohms (a ground path in ohm/kilo-ohm scale = short/grounded winding); OL terminal-to-terminal = open winding or overload.
- Voltage is electrical pressure between two points: zero volts across a CLOSED switch is normal (no pressure drop); you see voltage across an OPEN switch — a common mistake is calling zero volts across a switch an open.
- Amp clamps only work when energized and are for confirming operation, not why something isn't working; they become inaccurate at low current, which is why techs misdiagnose low-current condenser fan motors as high-amp-drawing.

### Prevent this Common Manometer Mistake  
*Source id: 4J_-GkWhHQI*

- A dual-port manometer (demoed on the SDMN6) shows two different measurements at once: the large number is one probe (P1 or P2) referenced to atmosphere, and the small number is delta P, the pressure difference between the two probes. You must know which one you are reading.
- For total external static pressure, read the delta P (bottom) number with one probe in the return and one in the supply, because you want the differential across the air handler.
- For gas-valve pressure, toggle between P1 and P2 to read each port's absolute (to-atmosphere) pressure (inlet as P1, manifold/outlet as P2); delta P is meaningless there.

### Reading Duct Velocity Pressure, CFM and Static with a Testo 510i and a Pitot Tube  
*Source id: db02zd_XLaA*

### Short 24 - Hard vs. Soft Copper  
*Source id: S47HEE6v-xM*

- Soft copper is for working by hand — bending, swaging, flaring — but it hangs poorly and isn't structurally sound; hard copper is straight, rigid, and hangs/looks neat over distance.

### Short 26 - Temperature Mistakes  
*Source id: wAd9HfwrVVw*

- Three big temperature-measurement mistakes: ignoring radiant heat transfer, failing to let air mix, and not accounting for surface cleanliness under line clamps.
- Heat-strip heat runs down the center of the duct and doesn't mix — you can read a normal 19-degree split with strips running while a mixed reading downstream is only ~12; on a furnace with a case coil on top the probe is close to the coil and reads low from radiant exchange.
- Outdoors, measure ambient in the shade away from discharge air and hot surfaces (pool heaters etc.); thermostats and probes both suffer radiant error from surfaces in their line of sight.

### Short 30 - Braze or Solder Anything  
*Source id: 4JLNdaL3QXg*

- To braze/solder any joint you must match the rod and flux to the base metal: phosphorus (SilFos/Foz) rods work copper-to-copper without flux but must NOT be used on steel; steel needs a high-silver rod plus a separate flux.
- Different metals conduct heat differently (copper spreads heat, steel localizes it, aluminum melts under ~1300F with no color change) — know melt temperatures and visual/flux cues, and manage joint clearance so capillary action pulls filler in without pooling inside the tube.
- The most common mistake is not getting the ENTIRE joint to temperature — techs cap-weld the edge instead of drawing filler into the whole joint via capillary action.

### Short 35 - Resolution, Precision & Accuracy  
*Source id: aap0f8aU7ZA*

- Accuracy = hitting the bullseye (true value, requires calibration against a reference); precision = a tight, repeatable group; resolution = the smallest change an instrument can detect.
- Setting micron gauges side-by-side shows precision but NOT accuracy — you only know they're accurate if each was calibrated against a reference standard.
- Buy the most accurate/precise tool you need with no more resolution than necessary; more resolution costs more, and displayed resolution beyond the instrument's accuracy (e.g. 0.1 Pascal display with +/-5 Pascal accuracy) is meaningless.

### The Science Behind Torch Tips Every HVAC Tech Must Know  
*Source id: mQCQuc95AW0*

### The ＂Heat Trick＂ for Checking Compressor Oil with Roman Baugh  
*Source id: H1gutEscT-M*

### Thermal Imaging Camera Training for HVAC Techs  
*Source id: 4yn2Wf9s3oY*

- Thermography is as much art as science — it's about comparisons ('this one's worse than the others'), so use the thermal imaging camera regularly to build a baseline of what normal looks like.
- Reflective surfaces (glass, shiny metal, mirrors) don't show their own temperature — they show what they reflect; only a perfectly non-reflecting surface (emissivity of one) is measured perfectly.
- You can read subcooling non-invasively on visible condenser coils: de-superheating (hot, cooling), condensing (constant temperature for a long stretch), then subcooling (temperature drops again in the bottom rows) — the more the cool 'stacks' at the bottom, the more subcooling/fullness.

### Thermal Imaging in HVAC： Theory & Practice w⧸ Bill Spohn & Eric Kaiser

### Use 45% Silver Solder to Braze Steel and Copper  
*Source id: q05-RUAZUkk*

## Contrarian takes (where Bryan / guests diverge from common teaching)

- **Common teaching:** Set torch pressures at 7 (oxygen) and 11 (acetylene) and fine-tune the flame at the torch handles.
  **Bryan's position:** Use EQUAL oxygen and acetylene pressures (commonly 5/5) set at the regulator, not at the handles.
  **Reasoning:** Brazing-equipment manufacturers specify equal pressures; adjusting at the handles instead of the regulator gives poor heat control and is unsafe.
  **Source:** [(Podcast) Brazing Basics and Tips] (id: Hcp_ExcxvO0)

- **Common teaching:** Unsweat and reuse fittings.
  **Bryan's position:** Don't unsweat unless it's an absolute must; cut fittings out instead.
  **Reasoning:** Unsweating risks pressure buildup, the refrigerant oil catching fire, releasing moisture into the system, and burning refrigerant gives off poisonous (phosgene) gas.
  **Source:** [(Podcast) Brazing Basics and Tips] (id: Hcp_ExcxvO0)

- **Common teaching:** Light your torch with a cigarette lighter.
  **Bryan's position:** Use a proper striker.
  **Reasoning:** A striker is designed for torches, is safer (fingers not near the outlet), and works when wearing gloves.
  **Source:** [(Podcast) Brazing Basics and Tips] (id: Hcp_ExcxvO0)

- **Common teaching:** Pig can't travel long line sets / pressure drop limits it
  **Bryan's position:** There's no theoretical length limit - it's static pressure behind the pig, not flow, so length/turns don't matter as long as you build the pressure column
  **Reasoning:** the pig is a blockage building static pressure, not a flow-resistance situation
  **Source:** [AHR 2022 LIVE Video Roundup] (id: YY3dbrd7IbE)

- **Common teaching:** Analog is all you need, always
  **Bryan's position:** Old analog gauges on modern critically-charged/micro-channel systems can be off by ~2 ounces of refrigerant, hurting performance - digital's accuracy matters there
  **Reasoning:** lower total charge means a couple ounces is a big percentage
  **Source:** [Analog & Digital Manifolds] (id: 1GE2yNIW268)

- **Common teaching:** Anything but digital is ridiculous
  **Bryan's position:** Not the whole story - analog still fits recovery, contaminated systems, catching valve/TXV jitter, and is cheaper; keep both on the truck
  **Reasoning:** different jobs need different tools
  **Source:** [Analog & Digital Manifolds] (id: 1GE2yNIW268)

- **Common teaching:** It's fine to learn straight on digital auto-calculation of superheat/subcool
  **Bryan's position:** In a school setting, learn on analog (or at least don't auto-calculate) - compute superheat/subcool manually from a PT chart to lock it into your brain
  **Reasoning:** over-reliance on technology (like GPS) means you follow directions without understanding
  **Source:** [Analog & Digital Manifolds] (id: 1GE2yNIW268)

- **Common teaching:** A pocket swaging tool is a practical everyday tool
  **Bryan's position:** Andrew calls the simple hand swage a get-out-of-jail service tool, not for consistent use - it's a pain and works you painfully; better for restoring damaged/stepped-on pipe or adapting sizes in a pinch
  **Reasoning:** it's impractical for regular production work
  **Source:** [Andrew Greaves Demonstrates NTB7L Power Tubing Bender] (id: BJuEsrhBZP0)

- **Common teaching:** When copper turns dark red you've burned it up.
  **Bryan's position:** With copper you WANT to see that slight red color change - it's the signal the joint is ready for the alloy, not a sign it's ruined.
  **Reasoning:** The globby, ugly joints come from techs getting scared, moving the torch all over, and never getting hot enough.
  **Source:** [Braze or Solder Anything in HVAC⧸R] (id: 1HLpV45pyY8)

- **Common teaching:** A flux-cored aluminum rod (or a pretty free-flowing competitor product) is the best aluminum patch.
  **Bryan's position:** AlloySol (external flux) makes a far harder, stronger aluminum patch - Bryan impact-tested it and it barely dented, while a competitor product cracked apart just while cooling.
  **Reasoning:** Many aluminum products are designed to draw into a joint, not to be a strong surface patch on a rub-out.
  **Source:** [Braze or Solder Anything in HVAC⧸R] (id: 1HLpV45pyY8)

- **Common teaching:** A plain water-soaked wet rag is a fine, free heat shield.
  **Bryan's position:** Use the real WetRag - a plain wet rag drips water onto boards/electrical and, as it heats, makes steam that blows out your flame (forcing repeated reheats).
  **Reasoning:** Steam/moisture forced into the joint and system turns to acid with POE/PVE oil if not properly evacuated, damaging the compressor and components.
  **Source:** [Brazing Heat Protection with Viper Wetrag] (id: bbgziEvklhI)

- **Common teaching:** You can save money using 0% or 5% silver rod, and tips can be snugged with a wrench.
  **Bryan's position:** 15% silphos is the tried-and-true standard - low-silver rod cracks and demands pristine conditions; and torch tips must be HAND-tightened (a crescent wrench squishes/destroys the sealing O-rings).
  **Reasoning:** A poor-quality brazed joint cracks later; a squished tip O-ring stops sealing and causes improper combustion.
  **Source:** [Brazing Required Practices with Roman Baugh Part 1] (id: ZvFiPGp6l0M)

- **Common teaching:** When copper turns dark/cherry red you've overheated it.
  **Bryan's position:** Cherry red is the target; ORANGE is too hot (melting). Keep the torch moving and adjust distance to hold even heat right at cherry red.
  **Reasoning:** It's a fine line between cherry red (ready) and orange (a hole); stopping or getting too close instantly melts it.
  **Source:** [Brazing Required Practices with Roman Baugh Part 2] (id: DPGiMU2diDw)

- **Common teaching:** More solder makes a stronger joint.
  **Bryan's position:** A rod-size joint shouldn't take more than about one rod - if you're three rods in, the joint is loose and you're sucking alloy inside and restricting the line; cut it out.
  **Reasoning:** Overheating a loose joint pulls an entire stick of solder inside, reducing the liquid-line bore at that connection.
  **Source:** [Brazing Required Practices with Roman Baugh Part 2] (id: DPGiMU2diDw)

- **Common teaching:** 15% silphos is 'too runny' - you want a gloppy gel to build a nice cap on the edge.
  **Bryan's position:** That runniness is the point - you WANT it thin so it pulls into the joint by capillary action; the work is inside the joint, not the outside cap.
  **Reasoning:** Beading inside the tube from over-drawing happens in soldering (plumbing), not in brazing with silphos as long as the gaps are correct.
  **Source:** [Brazing Techniques] (id: QBvANI3hyB4)

- **Common teaching:** Old-timers never flowed nitrogen and it was fine, so it's overkill.
  **Bryan's position:** It matters now because POE oil strips cupric oxide (mineral oil didn't); flow nitrogen - waste it freely, it's cheap.
  **Reasoning:** On a mineral-to-POE gas conversion the screens/filter driers suddenly plug because POE strips the built-up cupric oxide fast; oxygen and moisture are the enemies.
  **Source:** [Brazing Techniques] (id: QBvANI3hyB4)

- **Common teaching:** You can use your meter to discharge a capacitor because the meter's resistance/impedance acts as a discharging device.
  **Bryan's position:** The meter works to test for charge but is not effective at discharging in a timely fashion; use a proper capacitor discharge tool.
  **Reasoning:** The meter's impedance discharges the capacitor so slowly that it can take roughly half an hour to fully discharge.
  **Source:** [Can You Discharge a Capacitor Using a Voltmeter？] (id: H3eyBGy2x1I)

- **Common teaching:** Block the condenser with cardboard to raise pressures in low ambient.
  **Bryan's position:** The FieldPiece charging jacket is a better, more consistent method.
  **Reasoning:** Cardboard is hard to source, looks bad to the customer, and only blocks part of the coil creating hot/cold spots; the jacket restricts the fan outlet more evenly.
  **Source:** [Charging During Low Outdoor Ambient w⧸ Charging Jacket] (id: 5L0A0mq-btY)

- **Common teaching:** Bleed the leftover high-side gas in your hoses back into the low side when finishing.
  **Bryan's position:** Don't dump hose gas back into the system.
  **Reasoning:** What's in the hose isn't in the system; dumping it back changes your charge, and on critically-charged / microchannel systems it can overcharge subcool significantly.
  **Source:** [Charging and Recovering with measureQuick] (id: 32vCt2xQrp0)

- **Common teaching:** You should never stand on the top of a ladder/step stool.
  **Bryan's position:** On a 24-inch OSHA-rated step stool it IS allowable and its intended purpose to stand on top.
  **Reasoning:** The rule applies to ladders, not to an OSHA-rated step stool designed for it.
  **Source:** [Cleaning Large Coils with CoilPro] (id: bmP59Ke2SWs)

- **Common teaching:** Never use coil cleaner on microchannel coils.
  **Bryan's position:** Generally use water only on microchannel; cleaner only in extreme/needed cases, mild and thoroughly rinsed. Train even recommends the 90-degree tip with Coil Pro in their literature.
  **Reasoning:** Manufacturers vary (some say never, some say extreme cases); microchannel is easily damaged.
  **Source:** [Cleaning Large Coils with CoilPro] (id: bmP59Ke2SWs)

- **Common teaching:** Trust that the installing contractor put the correct piston in a newer system.
  **Bryan's position:** If a newer unit with a piston does not match the condenser, the odds the installing contractor put the right size piston in are pretty much zero, so verify it.
  **Reasoning:** Field experience with mismatched factory pistons on recent installs.
  **Source:** [Cleaning an HVAC Slant Evaporator Coil in Place Using Viper] (id: UzJvCAmDEUA)

- **Common teaching:** Microchannel coils marked 'use water only' are safe to just rinse with tap water.
  **Bryan's position:** Pastorello: tap water is heavily chlorinated and chlorine degrades aluminum, so rinsing microchannel coils with only tap water leaves chlorine residue that can eat the coil over time.
  **Reasoning:** OEMs did not fully think it out; their cleaners include a temporary aluminum corrosion inhibitor and a rinse aid to prevent chlorine pitting and white mineral residue.
  **Source:** [Coil Cleaning with John Pastorello] (id: AoG75swnZVI)

- **Common teaching:** Foaming coil cleaner is just smoke and mirrors.
  **Bryan's position:** Pastorello: foam cleaning is superior because it hits all the contact angles and stays in contact with the metal longer than a flat liquid.
  **Reasoning:** Soil builds bridges at contact angles; billions of fine bubbles reach all of them.
  **Source:** [Coil Cleaning with John Pastorello] (id: AoG75swnZVI)

- **Common teaching:** Stronger (more concentrated) cleaner cleans better/faster.
  **Bryan's position:** There is a bell curve; using too much detergent is as bad as too little because excess detergent only contacts other detergent, not soil, and takes longer to rinse.
  **Reasoning:** There is an optimum water-to-cleaner ratio at the top of the curve.
  **Source:** [Coil Cleaning with John Pastorello] (id: AoG75swnZVI)

- **Common teaching:** I've done this 30 years and never had to deburr / a quick pass with needlenose is fine
  **Bryan's position:** You don't see the problem but it's still there; needlenose squishes burrs and gouges deep striations creating copper shavings
