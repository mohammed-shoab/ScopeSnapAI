## Key technical points (Bryan's core teaching, by episode)

### (Podcast) Advanced Evacuation w⧸ Jim Bergmann  
*Source id: 03QPUIyPkT4*

- Full rig, soup to nuts: two large-diameter (1/2 or 3/8 inch) ~6-ft hoses, pull the Schrader cores with two core tools, no manifold (it doubles hose length and leaks), and the micron gauge on the side port of a core tool so it can be isolated.
- Hose care matters: rubber hoses outgas and are gas-permeable, so use dedicated vacuum hoses and keep them capped/sealed (loop the ends male-to-male) to stop moisture reabsorption; POE oil bonded to water is nearly impossible to remove. Nylog is an assembly lubricant only, used extremely sparingly and kept off the gauge thermistor.
- Vacuum pump oil: change based on contamination, not dogma; test the pump alone first (should pull below 10-25 microns), pull a bottom sample to check for moisture/sludge, cap the exhaust when storing but NEVER cap the handle vent screw (it will blow the pump apart), and dispose of oil properly.
- Decay: Carrier allows a maximum 1000-micron decay on 410A; best practice is to pull to ~250 and hold below 500 microns for 10 minutes (target 250/500, allowable 500/1000). Required depth depends on the application (cascade/low-temp needs deeper).
- Nitrogen sweep / triple-evac is only truly useful to entrain and move moisture and to decontaminate the gauge's atmosphere (residual refrigerant fools the thermistor). Always purge nitrogen from the liquid side through the metering device out the suction at low pressure (1-2 psi) so moisture doesn't condense and drop out as liquid; degassing is fast, dehydration is slow.

### (Podcast) Evacuation 101 w⧸ Jim Bergmann  
*Source id: rYtJrIyPS-I*

- The deepest vacuum is limited by atmospheric pressure (-14.7 psig / 29.92 inHg / 760,000 microns at sea level); because you cannot raise the pressure differential, the ONLY way to increase flow is a larger-diameter hose. Pump size is secondary to the rig attached to it.
- Build the rig right: eliminate the manifold, use two large-diameter (1/2 inch ideal) hoses, and use core-removal tools to pull the Schrader cores before connecting. This cuts evacuation from hours to minutes (~16x faster).
- Isolate to test decay: put the micron gauge on the SIDE port of a core tool with a brass/copper connector (never at the pump), valve the core tools off, and watch the decay stay below 500 microns. Reading 500 at the pump can be 1000-2000 microns at the system.
- There are no significant flows in a vacuum: you cannot suck a drop of water out; the pump lowers pressure so moisture boils, changes state, cools, and can freeze to ice and sublimate (very slow). Break the vacuum with nitrogen to warm/entrain moisture and re-pull.
- Vacuum gauges read by thermal conductivity calibrated for air/nitrogen, so residual refrigerant makes them read erratically or falsely low; break with nitrogen to clear the atmosphere.

### Bert Teaches Class #1 - Electrical & Recovery Tanks #professorbert #bertlife #hvacschool  
*Source id: Ilsc1ZYwxTs*

- Recovery-tank safety: tare weight (TW) is the empty baseline, refrigerant weight = total - TW, and you may only fill to 80% capacity, converting the tank's water-column rating for the actual refrigerant's density.
- A tank (or a settled system) with any liquid is permanently at saturation, so knowing its temperature and pressure lets you identify the refrigerant (R22 vs R410A differ greatly in pressure) - match the saturation to about outdoor temperature.
- Electrical safety review: a meter reads only potential DIFFERENCE, so a back-feed through a motor shows 120V to ground on each leg but 0V across them - always safety-check one lead to ground.

### Condenser Install 3D  
*Source id: dwrbJKlHw6o*

- Heat-pump condenser install best practices (central Florida): verify the correct unit/tonnage against manual S/J, remove Schrader cores to speed recovery, and pre-evacuate the recovery tank below 500 microns; use the tank water capacity (WC) and tare weight to avoid overfilling past 80%.
- Keep the system clean, dry, and tight: braze with nitrogen flowing at 3-5 SCFH, standing-pressure-test (e.g., 250 psi) using a high-resolution digital gauge, and pull a deep vacuum below 300 microns with a decay/isolation test that stays under 500 microns in 10 minutes.
- Charge only if line length exceeds ~15 feet (or per micro-channel/manufacturer literature); add refrigerant as liquid into the suction line while running, and put the Schrader cores back only after releasing the charge.

### Contaminant Controls System Clean-up With Filter Driers w⧸ Jim Jansen  
*Source id: 7YLq8UmjHxw*

- The only two things that should circulate in a vapor-compression system are refrigerant and lubricant; everything else is contamination, and moisture, acid and debris are the main players.
- Install a new liquid-line filter drier any time the system is opened/accessed for repair, when pressure drop is high, when acid tests positive, and after a burnout; put a new one in even after you think the system is clean.
- A suction-line filter drier is a temporary cleanup device after a burnout (leave 2-3 days, replace at 2-4 PSI drop, repeat as needed); permanently leave only a suction filter or plain tubing.

### Copeland Filter Driers： Product Overview and Installation Tips  
*Source id: OCSaUDz2fx0*

- Replace a filter drier any time you open the system, or when pressure drop exceeds 3 PSI on a liquid-line drier or 1 PSI on a suction-line drier.
- Standard operation needs only a liquid-line drier; burnouts need both liquid and suction driers (an HH core with activated carbon for wax/sludge), possibly changed multiple times.
- Monoflow driers must point in the direction of flow (toward the metering device on the liquid line); heat pumps require a bidirectional (BFlow) drier.

### Don't Overfill Refrigerant Recovery Cylinders The Easy Way  
*Source id: dYNgpgUTs-k*

- You can no longer always pump down: don't pump down micro-channel condensers (small internal volume, can explode) and recognize that scroll compressors will stop pumping and go into bypass when compression ratio gets too high, so finish with a recovery machine.
- Never overfill a recovery tank: the printed tare weight (TW) and water capacity (WC) don't tell you how much refrigerant you can add, because 80% fill of water at 77F is not the same mass as refrigerant at 130F.
- Always weigh refrigerant with a scale when recovering, charging, or making major repairs; write down the empty tank weight so tank contents = current weight minus tare.

### Ductless System Install and Maintenance  
*Source id: tjw-MdfsX08*

- Flare joints are the #1 ductless callback; deburr/ream the copper, put the flare nut on first and correct orientation, use a battery flare tool or manual tool to a stop, seat the flare to the nipple, use nylog as an assembly lubricant, and torque to spec.
- On a ductless BOTH lines are cold (the EXV is in the condenser), so insulate the entire line set end-to-end; thumb gum/cork tape are not equivalent to tubing insulation and uninsulated sections condensate over hours of runtime.
- Ductless holds very little refrigerant (this unit only ~2 lb) and is critical-charge; add by line length per the data tag (Mitsubishi 1.8 oz per 5 ft over 25 ft of liquid line); use the shortest possible hose or probes because hoses can hold several ounces.

### EPA 608 Core Prep - Part 2  
*Source id: gi-RkhawFGU*

- Memorize the key EPA dates: Montreal Protocol agreed 9/16/1987 (in force 1/1/1989); recovery required for motor vehicles 1/1/1992; low-loss fittings required on recovery equipment and recovery equipment must be EPA-certified since 11/15/1993; technician certification required since 11/14/1994; CFC manufacture/import banned 12/31/1995; HCFC (R22) same phase-out 1/1/2020; venting of any regulated refrigerant illegal since 11/15/1995.
- Refrigerant cylinders are regulated by the DOT (not EPA) because they travel the road; recovery tanks are yellow body with a gray top and filled to 80% liquid max. Refrigerants are heavier than air and can displace oxygen (asphyxia); wear safety glasses and gloves.
- Evacuate to 500 microns and hold when pulling a vacuum; system-dependent (passive) recovery is only allowed on systems with under 15 lb of refrigerant and needs a working compressor - modern practice is self-contained (active) recovery.

### EPA 608 Prep - Type 1  
*Source id: wZH058B3x54*

- Type 1 = small appliances factory-charged, hermetically sealed, containing under 5 lb (refrigerators, freezers, window units, vending machines). Recovery equipment made after 11/15/1993 must remove 90% (80% for pre-11/15/1993); practically, pull to atmospheric, valve off, confirm it doesn't rise before opening.
- You can retrofit one refrigerant to another (remove all old, weigh in new) but you cannot top off one refrigerant with a different one, and you cannot field-mix (e.g. can't make R410A from R32 + R125) - it must be certified in the jug as the end refrigerant.
- Take extra care with old first-generation refrigerants (ammonia, sulfur dioxide, methyl chloride, methyl formate) - don't use typical recovery equipment; and with hydrocarbons (R290/isobutane) - no torch until fully removed, vent only in a safe area and chase with nitrogen.

### EPA 608 Prep - Type 2  
*Source id: Mnl_KY-D59A*

- Type 2 covers high/medium-pressure appliances - most residential/commercial equipment; with Type 2 (since 1/29/1998) you can also work on motor-vehicle A/C without a separate 609. You may never intentionally vent regulated refrigerants (Clean Air Act, 7/1/1992). De minimis = small good-faith losses/accidents, not an excuse to vent.
- Leak-rate rules apply to appliances with 50 lb or more of refrigerant: industrial process refrigeration 30%, commercial refrigeration 20%, comfort cooling and all other 10% over 12 months, with required records. (A 2/26/2020 ruling rescinded the HFC leak-repair provisions, leaving them for HCFC/CFC only.)
- No mixing refrigerants and no non-condensables (air/nitrogen from failing to vent or evacuate) - non-condensables cause high/fluctuating head pressure. Recover liquid first (fastest); chill the tank; if water-cooled use municipal water; keep the 80% fill limit and use a scale.

### EPA 608 Prep - Type 3  
*Source id: CXMLkI1WMcQ*

- Type 3 = very low-pressure appliances (centrifugal chillers on R11, then HCFC R123, now R514A / R1233zd HFO). The low side (evaporator/suction) usually runs in a vacuum relative to atmosphere, so a low-side leak draws air IN rather than leaking refrigerant out; a purge unit on the condenser expels that air but also loses some refrigerant.
- Recovery/evacuation levels: recover to 25 inHg vacuum (pre-11/15/1993 equipment) or 28.9 inHg / 25 mm Hg absolute (post-1993). Pressure test low-pressure devices at no more than 10 psi (higher risks blowing the rupture disc).
- R123 is a B1 (toxic) refrigerant, so mechanical rooms need refrigerant alarms, mechanical ventilation, rupture discs piped outdoors, and at least one SCBA for maintenance; ASHRAE Standard 15-2013 governs refrigerant-leakage safety.

### Flammable Refrigerant Safety Basics - Kalos Meeting  
*Source id: -MWhE-QpcD8*

- Refrigerant safety classes: A refrigerants are non-toxic (all residential/light-commercial). A1 = non-toxic non-flammable (the old norm); A2L = mildly flammable (e.g., R-32); A3 = flammable hydrocarbons (e.g., R-290 propane, isobutane). R-32 is an A2L that behaves pressure-wise like R-410A; R-290 behaves like R-22.
- R-32 is a regulated refrigerant that must be recovered; recovery machines (e.g., Fieldpiece) are rated for A2Ls. R-290 does not have to be recovered (you can vent it) — but never vent it indoors (restaurants), where much hydrocarbon equipment lives.
- The 'new vacuum pump for A2L/A3' claim is largely false: follow good practice — purge/flow nitrogen to get residual refrigerant below the lower flammability limit before any open flame — and standard equipment is fine.

### Flammable Refrigerant Update  
*Source id: UNIaV_dh2tw*

- The AIM Act (federal) mandates the EPA phase DOWN (not out) HFCs by 85% — 10% cut in 2022 jumping to 40% in 2024 — driving the transition to low-GWP A2L refrigerants; you still need Section 608 to buy/store/transport/work with them and you still must recover them.
- A2Ls (R-32, R-454B, etc.) are 'mildly flammable' and behave far more like A1s than like A3 hydrocarbons (propane/R-290): they need much more minimum ignition energy, form flammable concentrations less readily, have lower burning velocity, and cause a less severe event if ignited — which is why larger charge sizes are allowed than for A3s.
- A2Ls cannot be retrofitted into existing (A1/legacy) equipment — use them only in equipment designed for them; the ASHRAE 15/15.2 install-service standards and UL 60335-2-40/2-89 design standards were updated in 2022, but adoption is uneven state-by-state.

### How and When to Use a Schrader Core Removal Tool  
*Source id: jsVwIvdDv-s*

- Remove Schrader cores in three cases: whenever pulling a vacuum, when recovering, or when a core is damaged - because Schraders are very restrictive and slow evacuation/recovery.
- The core tool lets you pull a core under pressure without losing the charge: engage and grab the core, back it out, then close the ball valve to isolate it from the refrigerant circuit before replacing.
- Never pull cores in or out under vacuum - you'll draw air into the system; always be under slight positive pressure or fully recovered (no pressure).

### How to Evacuate an AC system, Full Vacuum Procedure  
*Source id: JsnQeUSuUMU*

- Evacuation removes everything that is not refrigerant or oil - especially water vapor and oxygen (which cause corrosion) and non-condensable nitrogen (which hurts capacity); a vacuum pump does not suck, it creates low pressure so molecules force their way out.
- The whole procedure depends on a clean, dry, tight system FIRST: purge then flow nitrogen while brazing, then pressure-test for leaks before ever connecting the vacuum pump - the pump and vacuum are not there to find leaks.
- Connect the micron gauge as FAR from the pump as possible (e.g. leave the Schrader in the liquid line with a brass adapter): the lowest pressure is at the pump, so measuring there can show 500 microns while the far side is still in the thousands.

### How to Evacuate an Air Conditioning System (Fast and Deep Vacuum)  
*Source id: QEMZ2kJ6ML0*

- Evacuation pulls the refrigerant circuit into a vacuum; you are really just removing the atmosphere (14.7 psia). Below vacuum, water boils at a lower temperature so it can be boiled out (liquid water cannot be sucked out).
- Do not pull the vacuum through the gauge manifold (all manifolds have restriction); keep the gauges for charging but pull with core tools removed and the micron gauge on the far side.
- With proper equipment (big hoses, removed cores, micron gauge at the far core tool) a clean-dry-tight change-out vacuum and decay test can be done in minutes, ~10x faster than the old 30-minute rule.

### How to Flow Nitrogen While Brazing  
*Source id: N2eCbXCZ8kM*

- Flowing nitrogen while brazing keeps oxygen out so oxidation (cupric/copper oxide flakes) does not form inside the copper; that scale otherwise breaks free when refrigerant flows and clogs TXV valves and screens.
- Two-step process: first PURGE the lines (higher flow) to flush out oxygen/air, then adjust down to a low BRAZE flow (~2-5 CFH) so nitrogen just trickles while you braze.
- Remove the Schrader strainers/cores from the ports first, and always think about which direction the nitrogen enters, what it passes through, and where it exits (the other end must be open/dumping).

### How to Leak Test an AC With Nitrogen Pressure  
*Source id: JaSUDn2VU04*

- The nitrogen pressure test confirms a clean-dry-tight system after brazing/flaring; do it right or you miss leaks. First address the Schrader cores (replace any showing oil) and use fresh seals in caps and hoses.
- Purge nitrogen through the system (~100 psi, out the other side) - different from flowing (3-5 scfh) - then pressurize to test pressure and valve the nitrogen off; use core remover tools with ball valves and minimize connections (each added connection/manifold is a potential leak point).
- Bubble-test field-fabricated joints under pressure; for electronic leak detection add a small amount of trace refrigerant in vapor phase (well under a pound on residential).

### How to Remove (Recover) Refrigerant From a Running AC System  
*Source id: fROHlPXw_H0*

- You can remove refrigerant from a running system by connecting the liquid line to a recovery cylinder, but never pull the suction side into a vacuum (or even to zero) or you can damage the compressor.
- On a TXV system set charge by subcool: compare measured liquid-line temp to condensing temp to hit the target subcool.
- Evacuate the recovery tank below 500 microns (prefer 300) before use, and only fill to 80% of water capacity converted to the refrigerant.

### Installation Best Practices： Brazing & Leak Checking  
*Source id: -B7G1hc_KcU*

- POE oil is hygroscopic (turns acidic with moisture) AND scrubs cupric oxide off the pipe walls — unlike old non-hygroscopic mineral oil carried by chlorine — so flowing nitrogen while brazing and clean practices matter now in ways old-timers could get away with ignoring.
- Braze with a neutral-to-slightly-carburizing flame (not oxidizing) to avoid building copper oxide; heat the tube side first then the bell/fitting; get the base metal to dark-to-medium cherry then apply rod to liquidus so it draws in.
- Use a four-pronged leak check every time: visual inspection (mirror), soap bubbles, standing nitrogen pressure test, and a deep vacuum with a micron gauge.

### Micron Gauge on the Pump w⧸ NAVAC  
*Source id: d5hVlRj8PzY*

- Large-diameter vacuum hoses (e.g., Accu-tools TruBlue) show minimal micron differential/pressure drop between the pump-mounted micron gauge and the gauge at the end of the hose; smaller quarter-inch hoses, manifolds, or Schrader cores create a much larger difference between what you read at the pump and at the system.
- The NRP8Di digital vacuum pump has a shut-off solenoid that closes instantly on power loss so you don't lose vacuum or suck pump oil into the system.
- An onboard micron gauge doesn't replace a system-side gauge, but it's a great indicator to test your other micron gauge against and to verify the pump is performing.

### Misubishi Ductless Evaporator Coil Replacement  
*Source id: Q1maDrCTCJ4*

- Replacing a ductless evaporator coil requires fully disassembling the indoor unit (boards, drain pan, louvers, blower housing) down to just the back piece and coil; pump the system down first (to ~zero, not negative, to avoid pulling in outside air) to save refrigerant.
- Bryan's key addendum: do a proper standing/decay test on the evacuation — don't rely on a micron gauge on the manifold; put the micron gauge on the side port of a core-remover tool with the pump directly connected and 3/8 or 1/2 vacuum-rated hose, valve it off, and confirm microns don't rise over time.
- Blower-wheel set-screw placement is critical: the motor shaft has an outer rivet, an indentation, then rises again — tighten the set screw ONLY on the inner rivet/indentation, or the wheel is nearly impossible to remove (Bryan cited a case where they had to cut the shaft off and replace the motor and wheel).

### Molecular Transformator - Short #217  
*Source id: BZOg7tU7oQM*

- A molecular transformator (a CPS trademarked name) is a tube-and-shell (shell-and-tube) heat exchanger: refrigerant leaving a recovery machine runs through tubes immersed in a bucket of water (the 'shell'), which cools it so more of the refrigerant entering the tank is already liquid, speeding recovery.
- Recovery machines have small condensers on purpose — a huge condenser would be enormous AND would hold too much refrigerant that you'd then have to purge, so recovered refrigerant often comes out as a hot vapor/liquid mix that heats the tank and slows recovery.
- A compressor mashes vapor molecules together so they move faster ('more jiggly') but can't directly compress vapor into liquid without rejecting heat — that's why AC compressors are vapor-in/vapor-out and heat must be rejected to condense.

### NAVAC NP8DS Smart Vacuum Pump Unboxing  
*Source id: 3cA8uXzi-ac*

- The Navac NP8DS is an 8 CFM two-stage smart vacuum pump with an oil-test mode: before pulling a vacuum it runs a preset vacuum on its own oil to test oil cleanliness/efficacy (worn/contaminated oil won't pull as deep), letting you confirm whether you actually need to change the oil and use less oil.
- An 8 CFM pump effectively covers everything from large (40-ton) rooftops down to fractional-tonnage ductless — the real limiting factor to a deep vacuum is getting the Schrader cores out and using larger hoses, not pump size.
- Leave the gas ballast OPEN on a wet system (lets air in between stages to keep from contaminating the oil) until the liquid water is out; on a clean/dry/tight system (new line set/coil) you can leave it finger-tight/closed.

### NAVAC NRDD Recovery Machine in Action  
*Source id: HcnkpOEcbCU*

- The NAVAC NRDD recovery machine is fast largely due to a very large (double-row) condenser coil, which helps with vapor recovery; its DC motor tolerates low voltage / voltage drop better than a typical PSC motor.
- Use core-remover tools during recovery so you get free full flow and an extra side port for gauges/probes, and so cores are already pulled for the vacuum you'll do afterward.
- For most comfort-cooling residential/commercial recovery you only need to bring the system to 0 psi (atmospheric); the required evacuation level varies by refrigerant and by EPA 608 requirements in the US.

### Nitrogen Facts and Tips  
*Source id: Fxdj02IlqVo*

- Nitrogen is inert and non-condensable inside a system: it doesn't condense under normal AC conditions, so it just takes up space (floating to the top of the condenser), raising head pressure and skewing the PT relationship.
- We use dry nitrogen to pressure test (cheap, ~78% of the atmosphere) because it has minimal water vapor; water is a universal solvent that causes corrosion, and with POE/PVE oils moisture is far more damaging than with old mineral oil.
- Distinguish purge/sweep (displacing air at high flow before brazing) from flow (a tiny 2-5 SCFH trickle while brazing); pressure testing or flowing nitrogen does NOT mean you purged the system.

### Nitrogen and Torch Use  
*Source id: RfALBU6D1nM*

- You must PURGE enough nitrogen to displace the oxygen before you drop to FLOW; Bryan built a calculator because references only ever say 'purge until the air is gone,' and the math shows you must purge far longer than expected (up to ~5 minutes at ~100 psi / 20-50 SCFH on long runs).
- Flowing (not pressurizing) at 3-5 SCFH keeps positive pressure so air can't re-enter after purging; that tiny flow is only about one foot of water-column pressure. Use a flow regulator, never pressurize, or you'll blow joints apart.
- Application/'think brain' over rote rules: cap and inspect pipe, clean before cutting, ream/deburr only when orientation lets the burr fall out (dropping a burr into 500 ft of pipe is the bigger evil), use the right coupling so the gap is tight for capillary action, and mid-seat valves so gaskets don't deform when heated.

### Oxy-Acetylene Brazing 3D  
*Source id: 5SGqzus1cpY*

- Proper brazing sequence: clean copper, ream/deburr (without dropping shavings or over-thinning flare edges), purge air, then flow nitrogen at 3-5 SCFH while brazing to prevent cupric/copper oxide inside the tube.
- Heat the male segment first and get the WHOLE joint (or a full section) to dark/medium cherry red so the base metal draws the alloy in via capillary action — don't just cap the edge.
- Select the correct torch tip by pipe diameter and application; set oxygen/acetylene pressures at the regulator per the tip manufacturer's chart and aim for a neutral (or slightly carburizing) flame, avoiding an oxidizing flame.

### Piercing Valve Training (Step by Step) with Roman Baugh  
*Source id: BrY-uIiHUrM*

- Full procedure for a sealed system with no service ports: gain access with a saddle/bullet piercing valve, recover the charge, braze in permanent Schrader ports, pull a vacuum, and return to service (608 cert required).
- Place the piercing valve as far out from the equipment as practical because that tubing section gets cut out when you install permanent valves; confirm the correct valve size for the tube for a good gasket seal.
- Speed small-system recovery by pulling from both suction and liquid simultaneously and adding heat to the evaporator; pulling from one side can let the machine pull into a vacuum and auto-shut-off before the charge is actually out.

### Pressure Testing Large Jobs  
*Source id: 9VqlhaUQz5A*

- After brazing large commercial systems (many joints, supermarkets), pressure test with nitrogen to prove there are no leaks; nitrogen is cheap, barely changes pressure with temperature in our range, and lets you test hidden joints (underground, overhead, inside armaflex) all at once.
- Never pressure test with nitrogen on a system connected to live refrigerant - don't trust a ball valve or service valve to separate them; physically cut and isolate the circuits before applying test pressure.
- Match the tool to the job: for a long-duration test (new store, 24 hours to 30 days) use an analog gauge - calibration doesn't matter, you grease-pencil the reading and it just must not MOVE; for a short service test (an hour) use a digital gauge watching for a 0.1-0.2 PSI drop plus soap bubbles.

### Pressure Testing and Leak Checking  
*Source id: kJ3JDFGJhmc*

- The part of a refrigerant system most likely to fail from over-pressurization is the COMPRESSOR TERMINALS (metal through glass fusite) - and they're on the LOW side, because the shell of a refrigerant-cooled compressor is the lowest-pressure point (suction gas drops into the shell). Pressurize below the weakest component's max.
- When nitrogen-pressurizing through the liquid line, both sides rise until a non-bleed/hard-shutoff TXV slams shut (the external equalizer is the closing force); stay below that point (~120-130 psi on a typical split) so you don't trap different pressures that later look like a leak.
- Use a multi-pronged leak strategy - visual, nitrogen pressure test (temperature-compensated Delta P), soap bubbles on every joint, and a vacuum decay test; the deep vacuum that holds is itself the 'fourth' leak-detection proof.

### Q&A - Vacuum Questions Answered  
*Source id: CcLMKofJBHI*

- Pull the vacuum from the largest-volume locations you can access, with cores removed and big hoses; on a system that has run, refrigerant dissolved in the oil evaporates only slowly at the oil/vapor interface, which is why service evacuations take so long.
- One-hose method: one large hose with the core removed on the suction side straight to the pump, micron gauge on the liquid side (far from the pump), pull below ~300 microns, valve off, and pass if it stays under 500 microns for 5-10 minutes.
- On previously-operating systems, use heat (heat gun on the evaporator/compressor, especially in low ambient) and sweep nitrogen through (blow it through, don't just pressurize/depressurize) to add turbulence and free refrigerant from the oil - a vacuum boils water/refrigerant out below their boiling point; you can't pull liquid.

### Recovering Refrigerant w⧸ a Working Compressor  
*Source id: ArC5EyD0G0o*

- When decommissioning a unit with a still-running compressor, use that compressor to help push refrigerant — let a good amount of gas flow through first before starting the recovery machine so you don't kill it.
- Raising head pressure (setting the recovery case/tank on top of the unit, optionally blocking the condenser a little) creates the pressure differential to get gas out faster.
- Use core-removal tools and short hoses for fast recovery; recover from the high side first (pinch off the liquid line), and only then open the low side — the running compressor pulls the rest out quickly.

### Recovery Machine Speed Race NAVAC NRDD Digital vs. Appion G5 Twin  
*Source id: oNEZmrF6wU8*

- Head-to-head recovery race: NAVAC NRDD digital vs Appion G5 Twin, each pulling vapor from identical 40-lb-tare tanks (~28.3 lb charge each) into evacuated recovery tanks, run to 0 psi.
- The two machines were neck-and-neck while pulling liquid/high pressure, but as pressure dropped (lower compression ratio) the NRDD's larger condenser coil helped it finish faster — reaching 0 psi about 9 minutes sooner.
- Proper recovery practice: pull cores while recovering and use a good high-capacity liquid-line flare drier in place (omitted here only to keep the race a fair head-to-head).

### Residential Install Checklist  
*Source id: 2tnTnQkJnfA*

- The Kalos install checklist is a laminated tool to catch what you already know before leaving and to train new techs; it covers condensate (quarter-inch/foot pitch, tested drainage, float switches breaking R), duct sealing order (fab tape then silver tape then mastic), nitrogen/vacuum, and electrical code compliance.
- Nitrogen pressure test AND bubble test are complementary: a nitrogen test finds larger leaks but a tiny microbubble leak can hold for hours on a big system, so bubble test joints and come back with a mirror to catch the microbubble river; any pressure drop after stabilization means a leak.
- Size wire to the data-tag MCA and breaker to the MOCP, and protect humans first, home second, equipment third - an oversized breaker on undersized wire won't trip while the wire glows red inside the walls.

### Schrader Core Removal Mistakes to Avoid  
*Source id: Tz-LGOPwGk0*

- The core remover tool (CRT) can replace a leaking/bent Schrader core with the system charged, and is also used to fully remove cores for better flow during vacuum and recovery.
- Schrader cores have tiny openings that severely restrict flow, and the small depressor on hose/probe ends restricts it further - fine for measurements but terrible for vacuum/recovery.
- Never put a core back in until you've broken the vacuum with refrigerant vapor and gotten the system above atmospheric pressure.

### Short 18 - Can You Freeze Water in a Vacuum？  
*Source id: A8pJHsJQMT8*

- Pulling a deep, fast vacuum with big hoses and a good pump does NOT create a problem by freezing water in an A/C system; that fear is bad information that shouldn't slow your evacuation.
- For water to freeze in a vacuum you must first HAVE water in the system (itself a bigger problem), and in ~75-80F A/C conditions there isn't enough thermal-mass cooling to freeze it; freezing is realistic mainly in refrigeration where the case is already below freezing.
- Even if ice did form, it sublimates directly from solid to vapor under a deep vacuum, because water vapor moves from high vapor pressure to low vapor pressure.

### Vacuum Best Practices  
*Source id: 1fXAZf0w53I*

- On a new install you can pull a vacuum to under 200 microns in under 20 minutes using one big hose straight to the pump (no core) on the suction line and the micron gauge on the liquid line core on the far side
- Measure the vacuum at the farthest point from the pump (liquid line) so 200 microns there means you're even lower at the pump; measuring at the pump tells you nothing about the far side
- Molecules at deep vacuum behave like cars on a highway, not water through a pipe: bigger/shorter hoses give far more flow even though the port is only 1/4 inch

### Vacuum Practices for Large Jobs  
*Source id: YMoP7wl8a0Y*

- The real purpose of vacuum is to pull moisture, air and non-condensables out of the system (clean it), not primarily as a leak check - we boil water into vapor by lowering its boiling point and remove the gas
- For large/commercial jobs use large hoses (3/8 or 1/2 in), pull the Schrader cores, use a vacuum-rated core removal tool as your isolation valve, and place the micron gauge as far from the pump as possible
- Every system leaks a tiny amount at the molecular level; a micron gauge is accurate enough to show it, so learn to distinguish normal decay from a real leak by the shape of the decay curve

### Vacuum Pump Basics  
*Source id: AkXx4_xVJ1g*

- A vacuum pump's main purpose is to remove non-condensables and moisture; moisture freezes at the metering device restriction and, with POE oil, becomes acidic and eats compressor windings
- Most modern pumps are two-stage rotary vane pumps; vacuum pump oil lubricates, seals the vanes, and absorbs moisture (it is hygroscopic like a paper towel on a spill) - contaminated oil stalls the pump
- The hype around high-CFM pumps is overstated for HVAC; the real differences are seals, hose size/length, pulling cores, and features - a 6 or 8 CFM pump is the sweet spot

### Vacuum Pump Gas Ballast Deep Dive - Moisture, Oil & Maintenance ｜ NAVAC ｜ AHR Expo 2026  
*Source id: lm3SgwUvugA*

- The gas ballast's real purpose is to keep vapor (moisture) in the atmosphere being evacuated from condensing into liquid inside the pump on known-wet systems - it is not for priming and not needed every vacuum
- Vacuum pump oil bridges the microscopic air gap between the rotary vanes and the chamber; when water degrades the oil the pump loses its ability to seal
- Performance-testing your pump lets you find the sweet spot for oil changes instead of changing every job or once a season

## Canonical field stories

### The subdivision of wet systems (cap-and-fill the copper)
- **Setting:** ~2002-2003, large new-construction company where Bryan hired/trained techs during the 410A transition
- **Diagnosis chain:** Startups kept finding wet systems -> two causes found: (1) copper line sets pushed through PVC chases with plastic caps that fell off, collecting water, and (2) installers told to 'cap and fill the lines with water' (meant the drain lines) instead capped, pinched and water-filled the COPPER lines.
- **Root cause:** Water intentionally sealed into copper line sets plus open chases collecting water
- **Lesson:** The only acceptable repair is replacing the lines; always keep line sets capped and dry, and roll out micron gauges with proper practices.
- **Source:** [(Podcast) Evacuation 101 w⧸ Jim Bergmann] (id: rYtJrIyPS-I)

### Unidentified TXV-fouling rust inhibitor
- **Setting:** Residential AC market, repeated TXV failures a few years back
- **Diagnosis chain:** Repeated thermostatic expansion valve failures from a fouling agent that passed through conventional filter driers and was then caught by the TXV acting as a secondary filter, forcing multiple TXV replacements
- **Root cause:** A rust inhibitor included in a sourced component was contaminating expansion devices
- **Lesson:** The HH carbon-based core removed the unknown contaminant even though they could not identify it; the TXV is also a good secondary filter
- **Source:** [Contaminant Controls System Clean-up With Filter Driers w⧸ Jim Jansen] (id: 7YLq8UmjHxw)

### Grocery-store oil-change clogging
- **Setting:** Old grocery refrigeration systems switched from mineral oil to POE
- **Diagnosis chain:** After adding POE, strainers/metering devices across the store clogged because POE scrubbed cupric oxide off the walls
- **Root cause:** POE's scrubbing property mobilizing wall oxide
- **Lesson:** Demonstrates why clean brazing/flowing nitrogen matters with modern oils
- **Source:** [Installation Best Practices： Brazing & Leak Checking] (id: -B7G1hc_KcU)

### Recovering a known-bad TXV system to verify charge
- **Setting:** A system Bryan had ongoing issues with, needing an expansion-valve replacement
- **Diagnosis chain:** Recovered the charge into a weighed tank to measure how much was actually in the system, comparing against the 5.6 lb factory charge
- **Root cause:** Recovered only 3 lb 8 oz — well below the ~5+ lb factory charge, indicating the system was low and likely leaking (plus a known TXV problem)
- **Lesson:** Recovery can double as a charge-verification and leak-suspicion step; less-than-factory recovered weight flags a possible leak to confirm with a nitrogen pressure test.
- **Source:** [NAVAC NRDD Recovery Machine in Action] (id: HcnkpOEcbCU)

### The recovery speed race
- **Setting:** Cold Florida day, two recovery machines side by side
- **Diagnosis chain:** Both machines started simultaneously on equal charges; NAVAC hit 0 psi at ~13 minutes, Appion ~9 minutes later; NAVAC purge recovered 36 lb 11 oz vs Appion 36 lb 1 oz
- **Root cause:** Larger NRDD condenser coil performs better at low pressure/low compression ratio
- **Lesson:** Bigger condenser coil pays off at the end of recovery when pressure and compression ratio are low
- **Source:** [Recovery Machine Speed Race NAVAC NRDD Digital vs. Appion G5 Twin] (id: oNEZmrF6wU8)

### The hot attic pressure test that masked a leak
- **Setting:** attic air handler, line isolation testing for a repeatedly flat system
- **Diagnosis chain:** Pressure appeared to slowly rise (not drop) as the attic heated; the temperature-driven pressure rise offset the leak
- **Root cause:** A real leak, hidden because rising temperature raised nitrogen pressure about as fast as it leaked out
- **Lesson:** Reset the baseline when temperature/rain changes conditions; jack pressure much higher (250 -> 400) so a leak shows faster.
- **Source:** [Residential Install Checklist] (id: 2tnTnQkJnfA)

### Installers hydrostatic-tested the wrong lines
- **Setting:** A company whose installers were told to hydrostatic-test drain lines
- **Diagnosis chain:** Installers mistakenly hydrostatic-tested the refrigerant lines, leaving water in the systems; had they then pulled a vacuum in cold weather they could have frozen the water in the pipes.
- **Root cause:** Water introduced into refrigerant lines by testing the wrong lines
- **Lesson:** Freezing needs actual water in the system plus very cold ambient; both are abnormal.
- **Source:** [Short 18 - Can You Freeze Water in a Vacuum？] (id: A8pJHsJQMT8)

### Chiller barrel pulled in moisture in winter
- **Setting:** Two chillers in parallel, one left open in cold weather
- **Diagnosis chain:** An open chiller barrel pulled moisture from the air; with no ambient heat to help vaporize it, the evacuation took forever.
- **Root cause:** Moisture pulled into system in cold ambient with no heat to aid vaporization
- **Lesson:** In one moisture case in 30 years, cold ambient (no vaporization heat) made evacuation extremely slow — add heat.
- **Source:** [Short 18 - Can You Freeze Water in a Vacuum？] (id: A8pJHsJQMT8)

### The salt-market compressor terminal blowouts from wet line sets
- **Setting:** 2005 new construction, first R-410A/POE systems, underground line sets pushed through PVC without sealed ends
- **Diagnosis chain:** New POE systems blowing compressor terminals right and left → installers pushing underground line sets full of water without sealing ends → POE is hygroscopic and holds the moisture → terminal arc-flash blows oil out
- **Root cause:** Water-filled line sets combined with hygroscopic POE oil; failures happened fast vs R-22 which might have taken two years
- **Lesson:** Clean, dry, tight matters far more with POE and electronic valves than it did with R-22 and mineral oil
- **Source:** [Vacuum Best Practices] (id: 1fXAZf0w53I)

### Injecting water into a pipe and graphing the boil-off
- **Setting:** Training bench demonstration with a small pipe shot with 30cc of water
- **Diagnosis chain:** Pull vacuum → micron gauge graph drops then stumbles/rises as liquid water hits its boiling point and flashes to vapor → recovers and pulls down to 165-168 microns once water is gone
- **Root cause:** Liquid water boiling into vapor adds gas and raises pressure until evacuated - visible as a tick in the graph
- **Lesson:** A modern Bluetooth micron gauge graph visually shows when you hit water and when it's gone, and shows normal decay (gradual slope) vs a leak/insufficient run (straight vertical rise like a rectangle)
- **Source:** [Vacuum Practices for Large Jobs] (id: YMoP7wl8a0Y)

## Contrarian takes (where Bryan / guests diverge from common teaching)

- **Common teaching:** A vacuum-rated manifold is fine to leave connected during evacuation.
  **Bryan's position:** Never use a manifold during evacuation.
  **Reasoning:** It doubles the effective hose length (yellow + red) and its packings leak; eliminate it entirely.
  **Source:** [(Podcast) Advanced Evacuation w⧸ Jim Bergmann] (id: 03QPUIyPkT4)

- **Common teaching:** Change vacuum pump oil after every single evacuation.
  **Bryan's position:** It depends; clean installs can go months, dirty/contaminated systems every time.
  **Reasoning:** Oil removes gas contaminants and moisture, not solids; test the pump's pull-down to decide rather than changing dogmatically.
  **Source:** [(Podcast) Advanced Evacuation w⧸ Jim Bergmann] (id: 03QPUIyPkT4)

- **Common teaching:** Ductless makers' stepped triple-evac (10,000 -> break -> 2,000 -> break) is a required process.
  **Bryan's position:** Often unnecessary; the vacuum itself matters more.
  **Reasoning:** The industry repeats bad practices from people who saw a gauge improvement (really just gauge recalibration) without understanding why.
  **Source:** [(Podcast) Advanced Evacuation w⧸ Jim Bergmann] (id: 03QPUIyPkT4)

- **Common teaching:** Use one large hose on suction with the gauge on the liquid line.
  **Bryan's position:** Can be done but is not the best way.
  **Reasoning:** Hard-shutoff TXVs block flow with no pressure, so you only pull from one side of the system.
  **Source:** [(Podcast) Advanced Evacuation w⧸ Jim Bergmann] (id: 03QPUIyPkT4)

- **Common teaching:** A triple-evac / nitrogen sweep absorbs moisture like a sponge and carries it out.
  **Bryan's position:** Nitrogen does not absorb moisture like a hydroscopic sponge; it entrains it.
  **Reasoning:** Moisture will not bond to nitrogen; the benefit is entrainment plus decontaminating the gauge atmosphere.
  **Source:** [(Podcast) Advanced Evacuation w⧸ Jim Bergmann] (id: 03QPUIyPkT4)

- **Common teaching:** Evacuate through quarter-inch hoses (the industry standard).
  **Bryan's position:** Never evacuate through 1/4 inch hoses; use two large-diameter hoses.
  **Reasoning:** A 1/4 inch hose's conductance speed is negligible; hose length and diameter are the biggest restriction, limiting you to ~0.7 CFM.
  **Source:** [(Podcast) Evacuation 101 w⧸ Jim Bergmann] (id: rYtJrIyPS-I)

- **Common teaching:** A bigger vacuum pump pulls the vacuum faster.
  **Bryan's position:** Pump size does not matter when the rig (hoses/cores) is the restriction.
  **Reasoning:** 3, 5, or 15 CFM pumps all yield ~0.7 CFM through 1/4 inch hoses with cores left in, because the pump cannot exceed -14.7 psig.
  **Source:** [(Podcast) Evacuation 101 w⧸ Jim Bergmann] (id: rYtJrIyPS-I)

- **Common teaching:** Pull to 500 microns at the pump, shut off, release charge, done.
  **Bryan's position:** That's a huge mistake; you must valve off with core tools and watch the decay.
  **Reasoning:** You can read 500 at the pump and still have a wet system because the vacuum is localized at the pump and moisture boils off over time.
  **Source:** [(Podcast) Evacuation 101 w⧸ Jim Bergmann] (id: rYtJrIyPS-I)

- **Common teaching:** Pulling a fast vacuum with big hoses causes ice, so avoid it.
  **Bryan's position:** Ice caused by pull speed is very rare; ice from ambient below 32F is what's common.
  **Reasoning:** It's physics, detectable on a good gauge; if you make ice, break with nitrogen, warm, and re-pull.
  **Source:** [(Podcast) Evacuation 101 w⧸ Jim Bergmann] (id: rYtJrIyPS-I)

- **Common teaching:** A couple degrees of temperature change across the filter drier means it is time to change it (attributed to Trevor Matthews)
  **Bryan's position:** Pressure drop, not temperature, is the true indicator of a clogged drier
  **Reasoning:** Particulate debris accumulates on the filtering surface and creates pressure drop; temperature can only approximate it if you know it is single-phase and use a PT chart
  **Source:** [Contaminant Controls System Clean-up With Filter Driers w⧸ Jim Jansen] (id: 7YLq8UmjHxw)

- **Common teaching:** Just add acid neutralizer to the system instead of a suction-line drier
  **Bryan's position:** As a rule that is a band-aid; only refrigerant and lubricant should circulate
  **Reasoning:** Adding a third constituent creates possibility for things to go awry except in unusual circumstances
  **Source:** [Contaminant Controls System Clean-up With Filter Driers w⧸ Jim Jansen] (id: 7YLq8UmjHxw)

- **Common teaching:** Take 80% of the tank's water capacity as your safe fill.
  **Bryan's position:** That only works if you were filling with water; refrigerant density at 130F differs, so use the app's recovery tank fill calculator.
  **Reasoning:** Water capacity is liquid water density at 77F; the calculator accounts for refrigerant density at 130F and the 80% maximum.
  **Source:** [Don't Overfill Refrigerant Recovery Cylinders The Easy Way] (id: dYNgpgUTs-k)

- **Common teaching:** Never pressurize a heat-pump ductless above its low-side pressure
  **Bryan's position:** You pressure test the outdoor/flare side over 320 PSI (manufacturers often recommend ~400+) because in heat mode everything outside the condenser is high side
  **Reasoning:** In heat mode the indoor coil is the condenser; the compressor shell (low side / fusible plug) is the weak point you must NOT pressurize over ~320 PSI
  **Source:** [Ductless System Install and Maintenance] (id: tjw-MdfsX08)

- **Common teaching:** 'Evacuating refrigerant' means pulling a vacuum
  **Bryan's position:** In EPA literature 'evacuation' often means recovery (removing refrigerant to a tank); in the trade 'evacuation' means pulling a vacuum (removing air/moisture)
  **Reasoning:** The wording is confusing and shows up on the exam
  **Source:** [EPA 608 Core Prep - Part 2] (id: gi-RkhawFGU)

- **Common teaching:** You always pull a system below atmospheric during recovery
  **Bryan's position:** For high/very-high pressure appliances under 200 lb, or for a known leak or small repair, the EPA does not require pulling below atmospheric (though there's no provision for NOT reaching atmospheric)
  **Reasoning:** Common field practice of pulling to 1-2 psi isn't actually allowed in EPA literature; the 200 lb threshold changes the required level
  **Source:** [EPA 608 Prep - Type 2] (id: Mnl_KY-D59A)

- **Common teaching:** You need a new/dedicated vacuum pump to work on A2L or R-290 systems.
  **Bryan's position:** That's not true — as long as you flow plenty of nitrogen to purge residual refrigerant below the lower flammability limit and follow good practices, a standard vacuum pump is fine.
  **Reasoning:** Flammability requires enough fuel (above the lower flammability limit); purging with nitrogen removes residual refrigerant so there isn't enough to ignite.
  **Source:** [Flammable Refrigerant Safety Basics - Kalos Meeting] (id: -MWhE-QpcD8)

- **Common teaching:** R-410A is a great refrigerant.
  **Bryan's position:** What makes R-410A not great is the R-125 blended into it; R-410A is 50% R-32 / 50% R-125, and R-125 is a poor refrigerant used only as a flame retardant to keep the R-32 from being flammable.
  **Reasoning:** R-32 alone is one of the best refrigerants (good latent heat of vaporization); the added R-125 exists solely to make the blend an A1.
  **Source:** [Flammable Refrigerant Safety Basics - Kalos Meeting] (id: -MWhE-QpcD8)

- **Common teaching:** These new flammable refrigerants are scary and dangerous to work with.
  **Bryan's position:** A2Ls are only mildly flammable and are more like the A1s you already use; we already handle far more flammable things daily (piping natural gas into a furnace and setting it on fire, sitting on 32 gallons of A3 gasoline in the van, hauling propane tanks) — with proper safety built into design and standards it's manageable.
  **Reasoning:** The updated ASHRAE/UL standards and equipment safeties are the built-in safety, just like the rollout/pressure/limit switches on a gas furnace; a chemist notes 'if you were trying to make a fire with 2Ls you'd be disappointed.'
  **Source:** [Flammable Refrigerant Update] (id: UNIaV_dh2tw)

- **Common teaching:** Pull vacuum slowly or you will freeze moisture in the system, and pulling too deep damages the oil.
  **Bryan's position:** Both are largely myths: below ~1 micron would be needed to affect POE oil (you never get there), and copper/aluminum conduct heat in fast enough to melt any ice; deep vacuum is the right approach - time is money.
  **Reasoning:** Systems are designed to move heat, so ambient heat re-enters quickly; deep vacuum best removes molecules. If worried, break with nitrogen (triple evacuation) - the benefit is mostly recalibrating the micron sensor, not carrying water.
  **Source:** [How to Evacuate an AC system, Full Vacuum Procedure] (id: JsnQeUSuUMU)

- **Common teaching:** Quarter-inch hoses and a manifold are fine because the unit ports are only quarter-inch.
  **Bryan's position:** Use large-gauge dedicated vacuum hoses (3/8, 1/2, 3/4), remove the Schrader cores, and do not pull through a manifold; the port restriction is like a short toll booth while the wide highway (big hoses) still moves more molecules.
  **Reasoning:** Restrictions and leaky manifolds slow and shallow the vacuum; tests repeatedly show big hoses + removed cores are fastest.
  **Source:** [How to Evacuate an AC system, Full Vacuum Procedure] (id: JsnQeUSuUMU)

- **Common teaching:** 'I never flowed nitrogen my whole career and never had a problem'
  **Bryan's position:** You don't know you had a problem — improper brazing kills compressors years later and gets blamed on the factory or lightning
  **Reasoning:** Old mineral-oil systems tolerated moisture and didn't scrub oxide; modern POE doesn't
  **Source:** [Installation Best Practices： Brazing & Leak Checking] (id: -B7G1hc_KcU)

- **Common teaching:** Always deburr copper
  **Bryan's position:** If deburring risks dropping the burr into a vertical tube, don't — a dropped burr is worse than a small burr
  **Reasoning:** A burr in the tube can damage the metering device; needle-nose can flatten a burr instead
  **Source:** [Installation Best Practices： Brazing & Leak Checking] (id: -B7G1hc_KcU)

- **Common teaching:** You can 'burn up' a brazed joint with too much heat
  **Bryan's position:** You can't burn up a connection — it's either melted or not melted (you can burn up valves with O-rings/seals)
  **Reasoning:** Excess heat's real risk is blowing a hole or damaging valve seals, not 'burning' a joint
  **Source:** [Installation Best Practices： Brazing & Leak Checking] (id: -B7G1hc_KcU)

- **Common teaching:** Pulling below 500 microns with the micron gauge on the manifold is sufficient evacuation.
  **Bryan's position:** Do it with the pump directly on a core-remover tool, micron gauge on the tool's side port, valve off and run a decay/isolation test to confirm microns don't keep rising.
  **Reasoning:** A manifold-mounted gauge and pulling through the manifold don't verify the system holds vacuum; the isolation test proves it.
  **Source:** [Misubishi Ductless Evaporator Coil Replacement] (id: Q1maDrCTCJ4)

- **Common teaching:** POE oil turning into black toxic goo (needing new copper) is because leftover mineral oil mixed with the new POE/R410A.
  **Bryan's position:** POE and mineral oil actually mix fine; the black goo is created by MOISTURE left in the system reacting with POE via hydrolysis, not by mineral oil.
  **Reasoning:** POE is hygroscopic and, once contaminated with water, permanently changes into alcohols and acids; mineral oil neither grabbed moisture the same way nor changed via hydrolysis, so old-timers got away without deep vacuums.
  **Source:** [Nitrogen Facts and Tips] (id: Fxdj02IlqVo)

- **Common teaching:** A bigger gap in the fitting pulls in more solder/alloy.
  **Bryan's position:** It's the opposite: the tighter the gap, the more capillary action; use the right-size coupling for the pipe so the gap is correct, and don't crimp an oversized one down.
  **Reasoning:** Capillary action draws alloy further with a smaller channel; brazing isn't gluing PVC.
  **Source:** [Nitrogen and Torch Use] (id: RfALBU6D1nM)

- **Common teaching:** An old-timer says he doesn't need nitrogen because he just makes sure not to get the pipe too hot (the flakes come from overheating/the torch).
  **Bryan's position:** Cupric oxide comes from oxygen reacting with hot copper, not the torch; if you pipe-fit properly (hot enough to draw alloy) WITHOUT flowing nitrogen you WILL get lots of internal cupric oxide, so flow nitrogen AND get it hot enough.
  **Reasoning:** Sealing only the edge at low heat avoids oxide but means you didn't actually braze the joint; proper joints need full heat plus nitrogen.
  **Source:** [Nitrogen and Torch Use] (id: RfALBU6D1nM)

- **Common teaching:** Separate flux is needed and getting the joint to full cherry-red means you overheated it.
  **Bryan's position:** For copper-to-copper, phosphorus-bearing (thoss) alloys need NO separate flux (flux just risks contamination); and you must reach dark/medium cherry to draw the alloy in — a beaded cap on top is under-heated and fails.
  **Reasoning:** Capillary draw requires the whole joint at temperature; steel and brass require different rules (no phosphorus rods on steel; use flux on brass/aluminum).
  **Source:** [Oxy-Acetylene Brazing 3D] (id: 5SGqzus1cpY)

- **Common teaching:** Use a precise, in-calibration gauge for a pressure test.
  **Bryan's position:** For a long-duration nitrogen standing test, an out-of-calibration analog gauge is actually better; you only care that the marked reading doesn't move, and a leak shows as a dramatic 25-50 lb drop, not tenths of a PSI.
  **Reasoning:** Over days/weeks you don't want to worry about calibration drift, dead app/batteries, or who can read the device; a big pressure drop is unmistakable.
  **Source:** [Pressure Testing Large Jobs] (id: 9VqlhaUQz5A)

- **Common teaching:** The compressor is the highest-pressure point, so its terminals are the strong part.
  **Bryan's position:** Compressor terminals are the WEAKEST point and are on the LOW side - the shell is refrigerant-cooled and the lowest pressure in the system.
  **Reasoning:** Suction gas drops into the shell around the windings; the glass fusite terminals vent under abuse, so be careful pulling plugs and don't over-pressurize.
  **Source:** [Pressure Testing and Leak Checking] (id: kJ3JDFGJhmc)

- **Common teaching:** Pull a vacuum slowly or you'll freeze the water in the system.
  **Bryan's position:** You cannot freeze water in a real copper system with a vacuum pump - heat keeps conducting in; stalls are caused by other problems, not ice. It's only doable in a glass vessel (Neil Comparetto's video).
  **Reasoning:** Copper coils/lines keep feeding heat energy in, preventing freezing; the myth made people pull vacuums needlessly slowly.
  **Source:** [Pressure Testing and Leak Checking] (id: kJ3JDFGJhmc)

- **Common teaching:** Nitrogen absorbs moisture / a vacuum pulls the junk out of the system.
  **Bryan's position:** Nitrogen does not absorb moisture (it displaces and adds energy/turbulence), and a vacuum only removes non-condensables and boiled-off water vapor - never dirt, solder, carbon, or oil.
  **Reasoning:** A restriction is dirt/solder/ice, not 'non-condensables'; non-condensables are gases (N2/O2/air) that can't change state.
  **Source:** [Pressure Testing and Leak Checking] (id: kJ3JDFGJhmc)

- **Common teaching:** Never use two vacuum pumps together because they pull against each other.
  **Bryan's position:** With two good, pre-tested, matched two-stage pumps he's done it successfully many times to get maximum pumping capacity; the pull-through problem mainly happens when one pump is much stronger.
  **Reasoning:** You want maximum pumping capacity on big/serviced systems; matched quality pumps don't fight each other.
  **Source:** [Q&A - Vacuum Questions Answered] (id: CcLMKofJBHI)

- **Common teaching:** Refrigerant dissolved in the oil isn't a problem, so ignore it.
  **Bryan's position:** It isn't harmful per se, but you can't tell whether the outgassing is refrigerant or water, so you must pull it all out to reach a truly deep vacuum.
  **Reasoning:** Water (from a wet system) is the real threat; since you can't distinguish, treat it all as needing removal.
  **Source:** [Q&A - Vacuum Questions Answered] (id: CcLMKofJBHI)

- **Common teaching:** Test at whatever pressure and watch one gauge
  **Bryan's position:** Test above 250 with a probe on BOTH sides or the TXV closes and one side reads high while the other bleeds through slowly, driving you crazy; add through a manifold so you can see what you're adding.
  **Reasoning:** A closed TXV isolates the sides and a slow bleed looks like a leak.
  **Source:** [Residential Install Checklist] (id: 2tnTnQkJnfA)

- **Common teaching:** A deep, fast vacuum builds ice that damages the system.
  **Bryan's position:** It essentially never happens in air conditioning; stop using it as an excuse to pull a slow, weak vacuum.
  **Reasoning:** A teaspoon of water on a 300-degree skillet just vaporizes; A/C thermal mass and above-freezing temps make ice formation nearly impossible, and any ice sublimates anyway.
  **Source:** [Short 18 - Can You Freeze Water in a Vacuum？] (id: A8pJHsJQMT8)

- **Common teaching:** Throttling to a quarter-inch fitting on one end negates the benefit of a larger hose
  **Bryan's position:** Larger hose diameter absolutely matters even when one end throttles down
  **Reasoning:** Proven repeatedly; with quarter-inch hoses/through-gauges you may get under 1 CFM, while this kit delivers the full 8 CFM of the pump
  **Source:** [TruBlu Kit Unboxing] (id: M8RQCPMJD7U)

- **Common teaching:** If it necks down to a quarter inch, larger 3/8 hoses don't matter.
  **Bryan's position:** Larger hoses still make a significant flow difference even through a 1/4-inch throttle.
  **Reasoning:** Flow is an averaging of the entire diameter over the length of the hose, not just the narrowest point.
  **Source:** [Unboxing the New NAVAC NR7] (id: IWPP8AdefUs)

- **Common teaching:** If your micron gauge stalls above 500 you have liquid water and must keep pulling
  **Bryan's position:** On a service repair with a new liquid line drier and proper process, stalling at 1200-1500 microns is well below the boiling point of water - release the charge and go, you're fine
  **Reasoning:** 1200-1500 microns is far below the point of water contamination; you just have molecules coming out of the oil. 'We're so busy splitting hairs when we should be shaving heads'
  **Source:** [Vacuum Best Practices] (id: 1fXAZf0w53I)

- **Common teaching:** Put the line drier at the condenser / leave it factory-installed outside
  **Bryan's position:** Line driers should go inside as close to the indoor metering device as possible to protect it from weather, salt air, and whatever is already in the lines
  **Reasoning:** The metering device is the first thing the flow hits on startup; an outside drier is exposed to salt/corrosion and doesn't protect the metering device
  **Source:** [Vacuum Best Practices] (id: 1fXAZf0w53I)

- **Common teaching:** A vacuum should hold perfectly right where you turn it off, and any rise means a leak
  **Bryan's position:** Every system leaks a tiny amount; a gradual sloping decay is normal, only a hard vertical rise means a leak or that you didn't run long enough
  **Reasoning:** Today's micron gauges are accurate enough to see molecular-level leakage at every copper joint; valve manufacturers even list acceptable leak rates
  **Source:** [Vacuum Practices for Large Jobs] (id: YMoP7wl8a0Y)

- **Common teaching:** You need the biggest, highest-CFM vacuum pump for power (the Tim Taylor 'more power' mentality)
  **Bryan's position:** Kevin: once you get down to ~500 microns it almost doesn't matter what size the pump is because you can only pull moisture off the tubing walls so fast; going too fast can freeze moisture to the walls
  **Reasoning:** A 16 CFM pump won't pull moisture out of a 6,000 BTU reach-in in a hurry; NAVAC's higher-end pump drops to a degassing speed at 500 microns for exactly this reason
  **Source:** [Vacuum Pump Basics] (id: AkXx4_xVJ1g)

- **Common teaching:** Measuring vacuum at the pump is wrong because you should measure at the equipment
  **Bryan's position:** A built-in pump micron gauge is still valuable: it's always available, gives a second data point, shows pump-only pull-down, and equalizes to the same number as the far side during the standing vacuum test

  **Source:** [Vacuum Pump Basics] (id: AkXx4_xVJ1g)

- **Common teaching:** The gas ballast is to prime the pump when you start it
  **Bryan's position:** Andrew: absolutely not - it doesn't prime; it breaks the vacuum within the pump (easier hose removal) but its real job is keeping moisture vapor from turning liquid
  **Reasoning:** Priming a rotary vane vacuum pump is a misconception; when pressed, people can't explain what priming even means for this pump type

## Diagnostic reasoning chains

**(Podcast) Advanced Evacuation w⧸ Jim Bergmann** (id: 03QPUIyPkT4)
- Gauge fluctuating up/down ~100 microns rapidly -> the Schrader core on the core-tool side port isn't fully open and an oil film is bubbling through -> pull that core or ensure full depression
- Vacuum stalls -> refrigerant-laden atmosphere fooling the thermistor -> a nitrogen sweep recalibrates the gauge and the reading drops immediately
- Hard to tell decay from a leak -> the logarithmic gauge scale hides it -> linearize the decay curve (BluVac app) to distinguish

**(Podcast) Evacuation 101 w⧸ Jim Bergmann** (id: rYtJrIyPS-I)
- Micron gauges 'stopped working' after a week -> techs shut pumps off without valving and sucked vacuum-pump oil into the gauges -> proper isolation/valving practices required
- Wet system suspected -> valve off core tools -> if vacuum rises past 500 and keeps climbing toward the boiling point of water, the system is wet
- Gauge reads erratically/too low -> refrigerant-laden atmosphere fooling the thermistor -> break with nitrogen to recalibrate

**Bert Teaches Class #1 - Electrical & Recovery Tanks #professorbert #bertlife #hvacschool** (id: Ilsc1ZYwxTs)
- Identify an unknown refrigerant: hook a gauge to the vapor side, read the tank/system saturation pressure, and match it to the tank temperature on a PT chart - e.g. an ~80s-degree tank at that psi lines up with R410A, whereas the same pressure at 120 F would indicate R22 (>250 psi).
- In winter a cold R22 tank may read ~80 psi instead of the usual ~150, fooling you into thinking it's empty - weigh it to confirm rather than trusting the low pressure.

**Contaminant Controls System Clean-up With Filter Driers w⧸ Jim Jansen** (id: 7YLq8UmjHxw)
- Determine filter-drier pressure drop by installing gauges either side, or approximate via temperature drop only if single-phase and cross-referenced to a PT chart for the known refrigerant.
- After burnout: install suction-line drier, return in 2-3 days, check pressure drop; if above ~2-4 PSI replace it and repeat until clean, then remove and leave a suction filter or plain tubing.

**Don't Overfill Refrigerant Recovery Cylinders The Easy Way** (id: dYNgpgUTs-k)
- For an evap coil or line dryer change on a scroll/microchannel system: pump down only partway (~50-60 psi, don't chase deep vacuum) then finish with the recovery machine and tank, because the vapor-only phase is a tiny fraction of total charge.
- Never pump any compressor into a vacuum; pump to just above atmospheric (1-2 psi) if you can, to prevent scroll plate separation/bypass.

**Ductless System Install and Maintenance** (id: tjw-MdfsX08)
- Small internal volume makes ductless decay tests very telling: pass pressure + bubble + 10-minute micron decay and you don't have a leak (a 5-ton would decay less due to more internal volume; VRF needs multi-day tests).
- Non-invasive maintenance test: force full stage, take a temp split (25-30F, almost always ~28); a 20F split means a charge/other problem; red flags (warm suction, ice on the expansion line, sloshing at the air handler) mean hook up and check superheat.

**EPA 608 Core Prep - Part 2** (id: gi-RkhawFGU)
- Convert PSIG to PSIA at sea level by adding 14.7 (atmospheric pressure).
- You may use nitrogen with a trace of refrigerant for electronic leak detection, but you cannot add nitrogen on top of an existing charge as an excuse to vent.

**EPA 608 Prep - Type 1** (id: wZH058B3x54)
- If a small appliance reads 0 psi when you tap in, don't connect recovery equipment - you'll just pull outside air into the system.
- CFC/HCFC refrigerants exposed to a flame create toxic phosgene gas; recover fully and purge nitrogen; never torch a pressurized system.

**EPA 608 Prep - Type 2** (id: Mnl_KY-D59A)
- Equipment leaks are exempt from 608 for the technician (you can recharge a leaking system under 50 lb) except where a known leak exceeds the allowable rate on a 50+ lb system.
- Open-drive compressors that have sat can leak at the shaft seal from lost lubrication.

**EPA 608 Prep - Type 3** (id: CXMLkI1WMcQ)
- Charge with vapor until above 32F saturation before adding liquid, so you don't freeze the water in the chiller barrel; recover liquid first for the inverse reason (prevent boiling/freezing).
- You must pull a low-pressure appliance well below atmospheric on a major repair because a 350-ton R123 chiller can still hold ~100 lb of refrigerant at atmospheric pressure.
- Warm the refrigerant (heat blankets / heated water) to raise pressure to 0 psi for leak detection or to avoid drawing air in when opening.

**Flammable Refrigerant Safety Basics - Kalos Meeting** (id: -MWhE-QpcD8)
- To recover a small A2L charge without a machine: pull a large empty recovery tank into a deep vacuum (below 14.7 psia), then connect and open — the tank's large internal volume and the pressure differential draws all the R-32 in, often before the tank reaches atmospheric again. Pull the vacuum all the way through a ball-valve hose, close it, then hook up and open.
- To vent/remove R-290 safely indoors: wheel the unit outside and vent, or use a long hose to vent outdoors (some remains in the hose); charge is under a pound so it's limited.

**Flammable Refrigerant Update** (id: UNIaV_dh2tw)
- Flammability is determined by igniting a gas/air mix in a ~2L flask: if the flame propagates side-to-side across a 90-degree angle it's flammable; an A1 (134a/410A) can still show flame enhancement near a torch and generate bad decomposition products, so be careful with a torch around any refrigerant.
- If a system is locked out for a leak or a machine-room sensor indicates a leak, ventilate the area with a fan before entering — don't charge in; even non-flammable gases (CO2 at 15,000 ppm) can kill in an unventilated space.

**How to Evacuate an AC system, Full Vacuum Procedure** (id: JsnQeUSuUMU)
- Confirm pump pulls below 50 microns isolated with a micron gauge (change oil, inspect for cloudiness) -> relieve system pressure and remove cores -> connect large hose direct to pump, micron gauge on the far side -> pull below 500 (typically 250-300) -> valve off at the core tool and run a decay test (must not rise above 500 in 10 min).

**How to Evacuate an Air Conditioning System (Fast and Deep Vacuum)** (id: QEMZ2kJ6ML0)
- Pull to the evac target (300 microns) -> valve off core tools -> run decay test; the BlueVac/MeasureQuick app calculates the decay rate and passes you as soon as it proves you won't exceed the decay target within the time window (so it can pass in under a minute).

**How to Flow Nitrogen While Brazing** (id: N2eCbXCZ8kM)
- Nitrogen enters one open port -> flows through the coil/line set across the joint being brazed -> exits an open port with the strainer removed -> if the far end is braced shut, do not start flowing (no path).

**How to Leak Test an AC With Nitrogen Pressure** (id: JaSUDn2VU04)
- Pressurize on the liquid line and watch the suction side come up -> if it stops rising you hit a hard-shutoff TXV (closed by its external equalizer), so you must pressurize from both sides to reach higher pressures.
- Nitrogen pressure changes with temperature: use a nitrogen pressure calculator for long tests - short (minutes to an hour) tests won't change enough to matter, but 24-48h tests can, and a split system averages both indoor and outdoor temperatures.

**How to Remove (Recover) Refrigerant From a Running AC System** (id: fROHlPXw_H0)
- Overcharge example: 115F condensing vs measured liquid line = 20F subcool, target 10F -> remove refrigerant until subcool hits 10 (added back 12 oz and reached 107F/97F = 10F).

**Installation Best Practices： Brazing & Leak Checking** (id: -B7G1hc_KcU)
- Solder won't stick and balls up on a compressor stub => you burned through the thin copper plating on the copper-plated-steel stub; clean and use flux/flux-coated rod.

**Misubishi Ductless Evaporator Coil Replacement** (id: Q1maDrCTCJ4)
- Known EVAP coil leak (found days earlier, charge reset, 12-degree superheat, ~133 suction) -> pump down carefully to ~2 PSI/zero without going negative -> disconnect the two flare fittings -> replace coil -> pressure test at 300 PSI + bubble test -> evacuate below 500 microns -> release charge.

**NAVAC NRDD Recovery Machine in Action** (id: HcnkpOEcbCU)
- Weigh recovered charge vs factory nameplate -> recovered less than factory -> suspect leak -> follow with nitrogen pressure test to confirm.

**Nitrogen Facts and Tips** (id: Fxdj02IlqVo)
- Non-condensable (air/nitrogen) left in system -> collects at top of condenser, takes up space -> high head pressure and altered PT relationship.
- Pressurizing through the high side and watching the low side peg while the high side keeps rising -> a hard-shutoff (non-bleed) TXV has slammed shut (external equalizer is the closing force).
- Vacuum stalls on a compressor/evap-coil job -> flow/sweep nitrogen to create turbulence and free refrigerant/moisture entrained in oil -> vacuum speeds up.

**Nitrogen and Torch Use** (id: RfALBU6D1nM)
- Existing/live-rack work: keep purge/flow pressure BELOW the pressure on the opposite side of the valve (e.g. 5 psi low side), or on a system running in a vacuum keep it separate while brazing; pushing nitrogen back into a live rack causes bigger problems than any oxide.
- Torch tip selection driven by pipe diameter AND application: too-small tip -> can't reach temperature, builds a bead/'stalactites' across the top -> vibration leaks later; too-big tip -> wastes fuel (3 joints instead of 20 per tank).

**Oxy-Acetylene Brazing 3D** (id: 5SGqzus1cpY)
- No nitrogen flow while brazing -> cupric/copper oxide builds inside tube -> contaminates system, clogs screens/dryers/expansion valves.
- Failing to ream/deburr -> burr creates turbulent flow on the outlet side -> unnecessary friction/resistance to refrigerant flow.

**Piercing Valve Training (Step by Step) with Roman Baugh** (id: BrY-uIiHUrM)
- Recovery machine auto-shuts off while pulling from one port only -> not empty, system just needs to equalize -> add heat, wait, or pull from both sides.
- Recovered weight vs nameplate: significantly short = system was already low (diagnostic clue); more than nameplate = someone overcharged it.

**Pressure Testing Large Jobs** (id: 9VqlhaUQz5A)
- Suspected leak among several hidden underground circuits -> isolate/cut the circuits off the rack -> pressurize each with nitrogen -> the one that drops pressure is the leaker (much faster than chasing with a leak detector at the pit).
- Pressurize with nitrogen -> soap-bubble all accessible joints while pressurized -> mark pressure/date/time/temp on the pipe near the gauge -> isolate and let it stand -> recheck at the same time of day; a 25-50 lb drop is a leak, a 2-3 PSI change is just temperature.

**Pressure Testing and Leak Checking** (id: kJ3JDFGJhmc)
- Suspect a leak at the service caps -> bubble-test the caps BEFORE pulling them off (especially if you see oil) -> if no bubbles there, don't waste time, focus elsewhere.
- Old/serviced system evacuates slowly -> moisture/refrigerant is entrained in the oil and only outgasses at the oil surface -> blast nitrogen through (not just pressurize/depressurize) to add turbulence and free it -> pull down; test the pump first (below 50 microns in 30-60s with clean oil).

**Q&A - Vacuum Questions Answered** (id: CcLMKofJBHI)
- Vacuum stalling on a serviced system -> refrigerant is still outgassing from the oil at the surface -> add heat and sweep nitrogen through to create turbulence and free it -> continue pulling; if it's genuinely wet, the pump oil goes creamy and you run long with the gas ballast open.
- Micron gauge placement: put it on the far side of the system from the pump so it keeps dropping after valve-off (a gauge near the pump/hose shows an initial jump-up).

**Recovering Refrigerant w⧸ a Working Compressor** (id: ArC5EyD0G0o)
- With a working unit you don't care about, use the compressor: push subcooled liquid into the recovery machine input, keep the low side closed until the high side is largely recovered, then open suction — the small remaining charge comes out fast.

**Recovery Machine Speed Race NAVAC NRDD Digital vs. Appion G5 Twin** (id: oNEZmrF6wU8)
- Recovery performance diverges as tank pressure falls: at low pressure the compression ratio is lower, so the machine with more condenser surface rejects heat better and finishes faster.

**Residential Install Checklist** (id: 2tnTnQkJnfA)
- If the system was flat on arrival or history shows a leak, do a line isolation test (close old condenser service ports, cut and cap/braze at the air handler) before proceeding with the install.
- Check the vacuum pump first: hook a micron gauge straight to the pump; it should hit below ~100 microns within a minute - if it stalls at 300-400, change or flush the oil (run hot and flush 3-4 times if moisture-fouled).

**Schrader Core Removal Mistakes to Avoid** (id: Tz-LGOPwGk0)
- Removing a core with charge in system: make sure CRT grabs the core tightly; attach to port (no flow yet), tighten (don't over-tighten and squeeze O-rings), seat on core, twist counterclockwise to unthread, pull out, then shut the valve to isolate.
- Connect vacuum/recovery hoses to the END of the CRT, not the side; the side port has a Schrader for a micron gauge.

**Short 18 - Can You Freeze Water in a Vacuum？** (id: A8pJHsJQMT8)
- Isolate the core tools and watch the decay curve: straight-up line = leak; a rise that curves and tails off = vapor pressure (moisture) boiling off. Repeated pull-downs that keep reaching a lower plateau (sawtooth) mean you are removing moisture.

**TruBlu Kit Unboxing** (id: M8RQCPMJD7U)
- Vacuum-rated core remover tools let you fully remove the Schrader for free/open flow (a Schrader is a significant restriction); if you connect vacuum to the side port without depressing/removing the core, you only pull a vacuum on your hoses, not the system.

**Vacuum Best Practices** (id: 1fXAZf0w53I)
- If a vacuum stalls: didn't pull the cores, used quarter-inch hoses, used the manifold, or hooked up multiple leaky places - a core-remover-tool restriction limits you to ~0.5 CFM regardless of pump size
- Nitrogen purge before pulling, and heat (heat gun or crankcase heater) on a compressor drives moisture out of the oil faster because refrigerant/water is entrained in POE

**Vacuum Practices for Large Jobs** (id: YMoP7wl8a0Y)
- Change vacuum pump oil several times during a giant supermarket vacuum and start the final deep-vacuum segment with clean oil because contaminated oil kills pull-down
- Open gas ballast during the rough vacuum to keep moisture from condensing in the oil, then close it for the final deep pull-down to regain full efficiency

**Vacuum Pump Basics** (id: AkXx4_xVJ1g)
- If a pump won't pull deep: change the oil first (if amber/milky it's spent), then check couplers/caps for seals, then confirm gas ballast is fully closed
- Confirm pump operation before every vacuum by capping and pulling on itself - a good NAVAC pump should reach ~15 microns

**Vacuum Pump Gas Ballast Deep Dive - Moisture, Oil & Maintenance ｜ NAVAC ｜ AHR Expo 2026** (id: lm3SgwUvugA)
- On very wet systems, leaving gas ballast closed lets liquid moisture into the pump and can overheat it; open ballast keeps it vapor
- You can only demo water boiling in a vacuum chamber ~3 times before the oil degrades enough to require an oil change - because water in the oil kills the vane seal

## Field tips (the trick that saves time)

- Use 6-ft large-diameter hoses (3-ft too short/stiff); loop the ends male-to-male when stored; keep separate dedicated vacuum hoses.  *(id: 03QPUIyPkT4)*
- Use Nylog sparingly (about a quarter drop), keep it off the thermistor, and cap hoses/male fittings because Nylog attracts dirt.  *(id: 03QPUIyPkT4)*
- Cap the pump exhaust vent when storing, but never cap the handle vent screw.  *(id: 03QPUIyPkT4)*
- Test the pump's pull-down first; pull a bottom oil sample to judge contamination; auto-parts stores take used pump oil; don't pour it on rubber roofs or down drains.  *(id: 03QPUIyPkT4)*
- Remove Schraders when recovering refrigerant too (they meter/restrict flow); replace Schraders and add brass/locking caps as an upsell.  *(id: 03QPUIyPkT4)*
- Purge nitrogen from the liquid side through the drier/metering device out the suction at 1-2 psi; use a ball-float flow gauge because pressure does not guarantee flow.  *(id: 03QPUIyPkT4)*
- When reconnecting the pump after breaking with nitrogen, open slowly (1-2 psi on the system) so you don't blow the oil out.  *(id: 03QPUIyPkT4)*
- Connect two 1/2 inch hoses directly to the pump via core tools; cap the pump's 1/4 inch port (its only use is testing the pump's own pull-down).  *(id: rYtJrIyPS-I)*
- Use dedicated vacuum hoses and keep them capped/sealed to prevent moisture reabsorption.  *(id: rYtJrIyPS-I)*
- Put the micron gauge on the side port of a core tool with a brass connector, and make sure the core depressor is fully opening it.  *(id: rYtJrIyPS-I)*
- For refrigeration, turn on the defrost heaters during evacuation to drive moisture out of a cold evaporator.  *(id: rYtJrIyPS-I)*
- If the gauge stalls or reads erratically, break with nitrogen, let it warm a minute, and re-pull.  *(id: rYtJrIyPS-I)*
- High-resolution digital gauges (Testo 550/557) catch small leaks on a standing 300 PSI pressure test before you ever go to vacuum.  *(id: rYtJrIyPS-I)*
- Chain pressurized cylinders and move them on an approved cart; a fall that cracks off the head (with no regulator) turns the tank into a projectile.  *(id: Ilsc1ZYwxTs)*
- Store acetylene tanks empty on the van for safety, and respect oxygen - it's the most dangerous thing on the van because it turns a fire into a bomb.  *(id: Ilsc1ZYwxTs)*
- Fill recovery tanks only to 80%, converting the water-column rating for the refrigerant using Bryan's recovery-tank calculator/article (Google 'HVAC School refrigerant recovery tank').  *(id: Ilsc1ZYwxTs)*
- Recover in liquid for speed, and flipping the tank upside down lets you draw into the liquid side without the dip-tube resistance (small gain, only worth it on large jobs).  *(id: Ilsc1ZYwxTs)*
- Use an analog manifold for recovery in case the existing system has contaminated refrigerant/oil, and put a liquid-line filter drier inline to protect the recovery machine; inverting the tank (recovering into the vapor side) is a slightly faster way to recover.  *(id: dwrbJKlHw6o)*
- Loop the liquid line into the suction line and pinch it down (or braze the bare ends shut) so caps don't come off and contaminate the copper when pushing line set.  *(id: dwrbJKlHw6o)*
- Set torch outlet pressures with the handles open, around 5/5 to 10/10 (Bryan uses 7 acetylene / 11 oxygen); go for a neutral or slightly carburizing flame and heat the base copper to dark cherry red (~1200 degrees) to draw alloy in by capillary action.  *(id: dwrbJKlHw6o)*
- Install the liquid-line filter drier on the inside nearest the air handler to protect the metering device and keep it out of the weather.  *(id: dwrbJKlHw6o)*
- Once you remove the pressure-sealed caps, install the drier within an hour or two (especially in humid climates) because it starts pulling moisture from ambient air.  *(id: 7YLq8UmjHxw)*
- Do not install a filter drier in reverse or you will prematurely foul the final filter portion.  *(id: 7YLq8UmjHxw)*
- A brand-new drier's moisture indicator showing yellow out of the box is normal; it responds to ambient moisture and turns green/dry once installed in a clean system.  *(id: 7YLq8UmjHxw)*
- Size the drier to tonnage or larger; do not undersize or it may be too restrictive out of the box and raise compression ratios.  *(id: OCSaUDz2fx0)*
- A suction drier is always monoflow: on a heat pump place it between the reversing valve and compressor, or lock the system in cooling mode during a burnout.  *(id: OCSaUDz2fx0)*
- Use the HVAC School app recovery tank fill calculator; you could even write the R410A max numbers on the tank since they don't change per refrigerant.  *(id: dYNgpgUTs-k)*
- When ice-bucketing a small tank, periodically pull it out and re-weigh so you don't overfill.  *(id: dYNgpgUTs-k)*
- The app's under-load capacitor test now warns that a reading higher than the printed microfarads means a mismeasurement, not a bad capacitor (capacitors don't fail high).  *(id: dYNgpgUTs-k)*
- For three-phase, check voltage across all three legs on the load side of the contactor and use the voltage imbalance calculator (over 1% is a problem).  *(id: dYNgpgUTs-k)*
- Pitch the wall penetration downward to outside on gravity drains; verify enough height above the air handler for a gravity drain before cutting the hole.  *(id: tjw-MdfsX08)*
- Gravity drains beat condensate pumps (less maintenance, less noise, less water-damage risk); if a pump is required, break the S/communication wire through the pump's float safety or tie into the dehumidistat.  *(id: tjw-MdfsX08)*
- Clean the blower wheel and reservoir/screen on maintenance; avoid running cleaners/chemicals through condensate pumps.  *(id: tjw-MdfsX08)*
- Do run tests before cleaning on a well-maintained unit (time saver), but clean first if the coil/blower is filthy so readings are trustworthy.  *(id: tjw-MdfsX08)*

- On small charges use probes and small tees or short 6-inch hoses to minimize loss (de minimis).  *(id: wZH058B3x54)*
- Don't leave a piercing/saddle valve in place permanently - they leak.  *(id: wZH058B3x54)*

- Pull a vacuum on a fresh recovery tank (below 500 microns) before recovering into it; expect the tank micron reading to differ from the pump reading due to pressure drop through the liquid dip tube.  *(id: HcnkpOEcbCU)*
- In liquid phase use the machine's liquid setting to avoid overloading the compressor when starting recovery.  *(id: HcnkpOEcbCU)*
- Always shut off the disconnect and test for safety before starting.  *(id: HcnkpOEcbCU)*
- Use soap bubbles, not just a standing pressure-drop test; a tiny leak may only drop 0.1 psi in 20 minutes, so use digital probes/gauges in delta-P mode to catch small drops over time.  *(id: Fxdj02IlqVo)*
- You generally can't flow nitrogen through a hermetic compressor; flow through the components around it, or pressurize and release to create turbulence to help release refrigerant/moisture entrained in the oil.  *(id: Fxdj02IlqVo)*
- Replace line-set copper when you see corrosion from reclaimed/irrigation water (high chlorine) in the ground or from a nearby water softener dumping on the chase; even if not leaking yet, it will.  *(id: Fxdj02IlqVo)*
- On installs/service: run pigs (pipe wiper / Hilmor kit) to physically remove old contaminated oil, nitrogen purge, flow nitrogen while brazing, then pull a deep vacuum; then copper is copper regardless of age.  *(id: Fxdj02IlqVo)*
- Bench-fitting valves: you can still flow nitrogen by taping a hose into one end and poking a hole in the other; only a hermetic compressor can't be flowed through.  *(id: RfALBU6D1nM)*
- On the last joint into a reciprocating compressor or live rack where you can't flow, purge/blow nitrogen in, then braze immediately so oxygen doesn't return before the joint is done.  *(id: RfALBU6D1nM)*
- Set the majority of regulation at the REGULATOR, not the torch handle, so bumping the knob only slightly changes the flame; makes it easy to hold a neutral flame.  *(id: RfALBU6D1nM)*
- Heat the male segment first and get the whole fitting to at least medium cherry to draw alloy in; an oxidizing flame (loud, jet-engine sound) adds oxide inside AND outside the joint.  *(id: RfALBU6D1nM)*
- Always leak-check tanks/regulators/torch handle every time you turn the tanks on; protect heat-sensitive (gasketed) parts and mid-seat valves so seals don't melt/compress.  *(id: RfALBU6D1nM)*
- Never use oil on threaded regulator connections, especially oxygen (combustion risk).  *(id: 5SGqzus1cpY)*
- Turn tanks off and back regulators counterclockwise for storage; bubble-test all connections as a safety step.  *(id: 5SGqzus1cpY)*
- Base-metal cautions: brass (copper+zinc) uses non-phosphorus flux-coated rods (e.g. 45/56); steel (compressor stubs, some valves) must NOT use phosphorus/Foss rods — use high-silver flux-coated; aluminum needs special alloy/flux and gives no color cue (rely on the flux going clear/quiet).  *(id: 5SGqzus1cpY)*
- Inspect all joints with a mirror before and after pressurization; let bubbles sit to catch microfoam.  *(id: 5SGqzus1cpY)*
- If the copper surface is rough/pitted after sanding, coat both sides of the gasket with a light film of Nylog to fill irregularities and reduce a weeping seal.  *(id: BrY-uIiHUrM)*
- Tighten the saddle clamp bolts evenly (alternate sides); leak-check the valve with bubbles before piercing further.  *(id: BrY-uIiHUrM)*
- Do NOT remove a piercing valve after piercing — the copper wall is holding the charge; if tightening won't stop a leak, recover first, don't remove.  *(id: BrY-uIiHUrM)*
- Leave permanent Schrader ports brazed in so the next tech doesn't have to pierce the system again; flow nitrogen while brazing.  *(id: BrY-uIiHUrM)*
- Moisture in an R134A system causes acid formation and can kill compressors — don't skip the vacuum/hold step.  *(id: BrY-uIiHUrM)*
- Use a core tool on the nitrogen rig - one sealed valve has fewer potential leak points than a 4-port manifold full of gaskets and fittings.  *(id: 9VqlhaUQz5A)*
- Leave a compact, self-contained gauge on the system (even just a discharge gauge with a short hose stub), not your whole manifold set, since it may sit for weeks.  *(id: 9VqlhaUQz5A)*
- Set the nitrogen regulator to the max rating of the LOWEST-rated component in the circuit so you don't over-pressurize evaporators.  *(id: 9VqlhaUQz5A)*
- Log the temperature when you pressurize and re-check the temperature when you return (HVAC School app has a temperature-compensation calculator).  *(id: 9VqlhaUQz5A)*
- Bubble-test service caps before removing them; use your senses/common sense first - check likely leak sources (water-softener salt discharge on copper, bleach in condensate dripping on copper, corroding Chinese line sets) before reaching for tools.  *(id: kJ3JDFGJhmc)*

## Guest wisdom on this topic

- **Jim Bergmann:** The vacuum rig attached to the pump is almost more important than the pump size itself.  *(id: 03QPUIyPkT4)*
- **Jim Bergmann:** Degassing happens very fast; dehydration is what takes a huge amount of time, so nitrogen zips back out while bonded moisture is the slow part.  *(id: 03QPUIyPkT4)*
- **Jim Bergmann:** The evacuation rig you attach to the pump is more important than the pump; large short hoses, cores removed, no manifold, gauge isolated on a core tool.  *(id: rYtJrIyPS-I)*
- **Jim Bergmann:** Factory-evacuated, correctly-charged sealed systems (a refrigerator) run 20 years without ever attaching gauges; do it right once and you shouldn't need to check charge again.  *(id: rYtJrIyPS-I)*
- **Jim Jansen:** Contamination is the number-one culprit thwarting the operation of system controls; most warranty-returned valves failed due to contamination, not the valve  *(id: 7YLq8UmjHxw)*
- **Aaron Ward:** Splitting the S/communication wire through a pump safety over ~40-50 ft of run can cause communication problems; the 10-minute vacuum decay/micron test is the best tell for tiny flare leaks  *(id: tjw-MdfsX08)*
- **Dr. Chuck Allgood:** A2Ls are much closer to A1s than to A3s — hard to ignite, poor flame if they do, and less damage — which is the whole reason the HVAC industry went to 2Ls instead of 3s; the science behind burning velocity, minimum ignition energy, and heat of combustion is what shaped the safety standards and codes.  *(id: UNIaV_dh2tw)*
- **Don Gillis:** Don't retrofit A2Ls into legacy equipment — it's not worth the liability even with tiny flame propagation, and you don't know what you're leaving for the next tech; he predicts the 410A-to-454B transition will be smoother than R-22-to-410A was.  *(id: UNIaV_dh2tw)*
- **Jason Obrzut:** None of the panel wrote this legislation — the AIM Act came from above and there's no turning back; the job now is to translate the codes/standards into plain language and build the required safety into installation, service, and design.  *(id: UNIaV_dh2tw)*
